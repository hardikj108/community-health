# Chapter 9 — Gender Health

*MPT Community Physiotherapy · Long-Answer Model Notes*

Gender health is the branch of community physiotherapy that applies a **life-course** and **gender-sensitive** lens to health promotion, disease prevention and rehabilitation, recognising that biological sex, gender identity, and the social determinants attached to being a man, woman or third-gender person profoundly shape health risks, health-seeking behaviour and access to care. The World Health Organization (WHO) draws a working distinction between **sex** (biological attributes) and **gender** (socially constructed roles, behaviours and identities), and its *Global Strategy for Women's, Children's and Adolescents' Health (2016–2030)* and *Gender, Equity and Human Rights* framework position gender-responsive care as central to universal health coverage. For the community physiotherapist, gender health spans adolescent health promotion, screening and lifestyle-disease education, pelvic floor rehabilitation across the lifespan, antenatal and postnatal fitness, continence rehabilitation, and inclusive, affirmative care for men, women and transgender/third-gender persons.

This chapter equips the postgraduate physiotherapist to deliver evidence-based, life-course, and inclusive gender-health interventions grounded in WHO adolescent-health strategy, Cochrane pelvic-floor-muscle-training (PFMT) evidence, ACOG/RCOG antenatal–postnatal exercise guidance, the International Continence Society (ICS) classification of dysfunction, and rights-based inclusive care under India's Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2019. It is anchored in Indian programmes — the **Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK)**, **Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK)**, **Anaemia Mukt Bharat (AMB)**, **Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY)** and **Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK)**, and **NP-NCD** screening. The chapter answers six 20-mark long-answer questions:

- **Qa.** Health Promotion in Adolescents
- **Qb.** Screening and Education Programs for Lifestyle Diseases
- **Qc.** Pelvic Floor Dysfunction and Management Across the Lifespan
- **Qd.** Exercise Programs for Improvement of Antenatal and Postnatal Health and Fitness
- **Qe.** Management of Urinary and Bowel Incontinence and Other Dysfunctions
- **Qf.** Exercise Interventions for Health and Fitness Specific to Gender Concerns (Men, Women, and Third Gender)

## Qa. Discuss the principles, frameworks and strategies of health promotion in adolescents, and the role of the community physiotherapist. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Adolescence**, defined by the WHO as the period from **10 to 19 years**, is a distinct developmental phase of accelerated physical, cognitive, emotional and social change — a "second window of opportunity" after the first 1000 days for shaping lifelong health. The WHO extends the concept of **"young people"** to 10–24 years and **"youth"** to 15–24 years. Adolescence is characterised by pubertal maturation, the growth spurt, neurodevelopmental remodelling of the prefrontal cortex, identity formation, and the consolidation of health behaviours — diet, physical activity, sleep, substance use and sexual behaviour — that track into adulthood.

**Health promotion**, per the WHO **Ottawa Charter (1986)**, is *"the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health."* It operates upstream of clinical care, addressing determinants rather than established disease. Applied to adolescents, health promotion aims to build **health literacy**, foster **positive youth development**, prevent risk behaviours, and create supportive environments (family, school, community, digital) that make the healthy choice the easy choice.

**Adolescent health** is thus a life-course investment: the WHO's *Global Accelerated Action for the Health of Adolescents (AA-HA!, 2017; 2nd edition 2023)* frames it as a "triple dividend" — benefits now, into future adult life, and for the next generation. For the community physiotherapist, adolescent health promotion centres on **physical activity, musculoskeletal health, posture and ergonomics, injury prevention, healthy weight, and mental-wellbeing through movement**, delivered through schools, youth-friendly health services and communities.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

Adolescent health promotion is theory-driven; the physiotherapist should be able to name and apply the following:

- **Ottawa Charter (WHO, 1986)** — five action areas: build healthy public policy, create supportive environments, strengthen community action, develop personal skills, reorient health services. This is the master framework.
- **Social–Ecological Model (Bronfenbrenner; McLeroy et al., 1988)** — behaviour is shaped at five nested levels: intrapersonal, interpersonal, organisational, community and public policy. Effective adolescent programmes act at multiple levels simultaneously.
- **Health Belief Model (Rosenstock)** — behaviour change depends on perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action and self-efficacy.
- **Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura)** — emphasises **self-efficacy**, observational learning and reciprocal determinism; peer modelling is especially potent in adolescence.
- **Transtheoretical (Stages of Change) Model (Prochaska & DiClemente)** — precontemplation → contemplation → preparation → action → maintenance; interventions are matched to stage.
- **Positive Youth Development (PYD) / asset-based approach** — the "5 Cs" (competence, confidence, connection, character, caring) focus on building strengths rather than only reducing risk.
- **PRECEDE–PROCEED model (Green & Kreuter)** — a planning-and-evaluation framework: diagnose predisposing, reinforcing and enabling factors before implementing, then evaluate process, impact and outcome.
- **RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance)** — for evaluating whether a school- or community-based programme actually reaches and sustains its target population.
- **WHO Health-Promoting Schools (HPS) framework** — a whole-school settings approach integrating curriculum, environment and services.

*Figure: The social–ecological model can be visualised as concentric rings — the adolescent at the centre, surrounded by family/peers, then school, then community, then society/policy — with physiotherapy-led interventions (physical-activity curricula, ergonomics education, injury-prevention policy) mapped onto each ring.*

### 3. The Rationale — Why Invest in Adolescent Health Promotion

Adolescents constitute roughly one-sixth of the world's population (about 1.2–1.3 billion), and India has the largest adolescent population of any country (approximately 253 million, ~20% of the national population). The **epidemiological argument** is compelling: many of the behaviours and conditions that drive the adult non-communicable disease (NCD) burden — physical inactivity, poor diet, obesity, tobacco and alcohol initiation, insufficient sleep — are established during adolescence. Globally, over **80% of adolescents do not meet the WHO recommendation of ≥60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) daily**, with girls consistently less active than boys. Adolescence is also the peak period of onset for mental-health disorders (half of all lifetime mental disorders begin by mid-adolescence), sports and road-traffic injuries, and the emergence of musculoskeletal complaints such as adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, non-specific back and neck pain (increasingly linked to prolonged screen and device use), and overuse injuries.

### 4. Priority Content Areas for the Physiotherapist

#### 4.1 Physical activity promotion

The cornerstone. The WHO 2020 *Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour* recommend that children and adolescents (5–17 y) accumulate **an average of 60 minutes/day of MVPA across the week**, predominantly aerobic, with **vigorous activity and muscle- and bone-strengthening activity on at least 3 days/week**, and that sedentary/recreational screen time be limited. The physiotherapist designs age-appropriate, enjoyable, inclusive activity — play, active transport, sport, resistance training with correct technique — and addresses barriers (safety, access, gender norms limiting girls' participation).

#### 4.2 Musculoskeletal health, posture and ergonomics

"Text neck," backpack-related loading (loads should not exceed ~10–15% of body weight), prolonged unsupported sitting, and gaming posture are growing concerns. Physiotherapists deliver posture and ergonomics education, screen for **adolescent idiopathic scoliosis** (Adam's forward-bend test, scoliometer), and provide guidance on safe study and device ergonomics.

#### 4.3 Injury prevention and safe sport

Neuromuscular training programmes (e.g., **FIFA 11+**, dynamic warm-ups) reduce sports injuries including ACL injury, particularly in adolescent girls. Physiotherapists advise on graded training load, avoidance of early single-sport specialisation, and management of growth-plate/apophyseal conditions (Osgood–Schlatter, Sever's).

#### 4.4 Healthy weight, bone health and nutrition linkage

Weight-bearing and resistance exercise during adolescence maximises **peak bone mass** — a key determinant of later osteoporosis risk. Physiotherapists reinforce activity-nutrition synergy and address both under-nutrition/anaemia and rising adolescent overweight/obesity.

#### 4.5 Mental wellbeing through movement

Regular physical activity improves mood, reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms, and enhances sleep and self-esteem in adolescents. Group and peer-based activity also builds social connection.

#### 4.6 Sexual and reproductive health, substance use — the multidisciplinary link

While not the physiotherapist's primary domain, adolescent health promotion is inherently multisectoral; the physiotherapist works within a team addressing menstrual health, SRH, and substance-use prevention, and refers appropriately.

### 5. Delivery Settings and Strategies

| Setting | Strategy | Physiotherapist's contribution |
|---------|----------|-------------------------------|
| **Schools** (Health-Promoting Schools) | Curriculum integration, quality physical education, active breaks, ergonomics of classrooms | Physical-activity curricula, posture/ergonomics sessions, scoliosis screening, injury-prevention warm-ups |
| **Youth-friendly health services** | Confidential, non-judgemental, accessible clinics | MSK assessment, activity counselling, rehabilitation of sports injuries |
| **Community & sport clubs** | Peer-led programmes, safe play spaces | Coaching safe training loads, neuromuscular injury-prevention programmes |
| **Digital / mHealth** | Apps, wearables, social media campaigns | App-guided activity, tele-education, gamified movement challenges |
| **Family** | Parental engagement, role-modelling | Family-based activity advice |

Effective programmes are **multi-level** (per the social–ecological model), **participatory** (adolescents co-design — "nothing about us without us"), **gender-sensitive** (deliberately addressing girls' lower activity), **equity-focused**, and **evaluated** with RE-AIM/PRECEDE–PROCEED.

## Qb. Describe the design, delivery and evaluation of screening and education programmes for lifestyle diseases, highlighting gender considerations and the role of the community physiotherapist. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Lifestyle diseases**, more precisely termed **non-communicable diseases (NCDs)** or chronic diseases, are conditions largely driven by modifiable behavioural risk factors — physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, tobacco use, and harmful alcohol use — acting through intermediate ("metabolic") risk factors: raised blood pressure, overweight/obesity, hyperglycaemia and dyslipidaemia. The four major NCDs are **cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes mellitus, chronic respiratory disease, and cancer**; musculoskeletal disorders and mental illness are increasingly included. NCDs cause about **74% of all deaths globally** (WHO), and the burden is disproportionately borne by low- and middle-income countries, where onset is often a decade earlier than in high-income countries.

**Screening**, per the classic **Wilson & Jungner criteria (WHO, 1968)**, is *"the presumptive identification of unrecognised disease or defect by the application of tests, examinations or other procedures that can be applied rapidly to sort out apparently well persons who probably have a disease from those who probably do not."* Screening is not diagnosis; positive screens require confirmatory testing. **Health education** is the planned provision of learning experiences to build the knowledge, attitudes, skills and self-efficacy needed to adopt and sustain healthy behaviours.

For the community physiotherapist, this domain sits squarely in **primordial and primary prevention** (preventing risk factors from developing, and preventing disease in those with risk factors), and in **secondary prevention** (early detection through screening), with exercise prescription and behaviour-change counselling as the core physiotherapy contributions.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **Levels of prevention (Leavell & Clark):** primordial (prevent risk factors emerging), primary (health promotion + specific protection), secondary (early diagnosis/screening + prompt treatment), tertiary (rehabilitation, limit disability). Screening = secondary; education = primordial/primary.
- **Wilson & Jungner (1968) / WHO consolidated screening principles (2020):** the disease should be important, have a recognisable latent stage, an acceptable and valid test, an accepted treatment, and screening should be cost-effective and continuous.
- **Population vs high-risk strategy (Rose's prevention paradox):** a small risk-reduction across a whole population may prevent more disease than large reductions in a few high-risk individuals; effective programmes combine both.
- **Health Belief Model, Social Cognitive Theory, Transtheoretical Model, COM-B / Behaviour Change Wheel (Michie)** — for the education/behaviour-change component.
- **PRECEDE–PROCEED and RE-AIM** — for programme planning and evaluation.
- **WHO "Best Buys" and PEN (Package of Essential NCD interventions)** — cost-effective population and clinical interventions for NCDs in primary care.
- **ICF** — locates the physiotherapist's outcomes at body-function, activity and participation levels.

### 3. Screening Test Characteristics — the Evidence Base

A screening test must be **valid** (sensitivity — detecting true cases; specificity — correctly excluding non-cases), **reliable** (reproducible), **acceptable**, **safe**, **simple** and **cheap**. The **positive predictive value** depends on disease prevalence in the screened population. Physiotherapists should understand these to interpret and counsel on results and to avoid the harms of over-screening (false positives, anxiety, over-treatment).

### 4. Common Lifestyle-Disease Screening Tools and Physiotherapy Relevance

| Condition | Screening/risk tool | Cut-off / method | Physiotherapy relevance |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|--------------------------|
| Overweight/obesity | Body Mass Index (BMI); waist circumference; waist–hip ratio | Asian BMI cut-offs: overweight ≥23, obese ≥25 kg/m² (WHO Asia-Pacific); waist ♂ ≥90 cm, ♀ ≥80 cm | Exercise prescription, weight management |
| Hypertension | Blood-pressure measurement | ≥140/90 mmHg (office) | Aerobic + resistance exercise lowers BP |
| Diabetes / prediabetes | Fasting/random blood glucose, HbA1c; **Indian Diabetes Risk Score (IDRS)** | HbA1c ≥6.5%; IDRS ≥60 high risk | Structured exercise improves glycaemic control |
| Dyslipidaemia | Lipid profile | Total chol, LDL, HDL, TG | Exercise raises HDL, lowers TG |
| Cardiovascular risk | **WHO/ISH or non-lab risk charts**, PAR-Q+ before exercise | 10-year CVD risk | Cardiac risk stratification before prescription |
| Physical inactivity | GPAQ / IPAQ questionnaires; **PAR-Q+** | METs-min/week | Baseline for activity prescription |
| Tobacco/alcohol | AUDIT, Fagerström | Screening scores | Brief advice, referral |
| Functional/MSK | 6-minute walk, sit-to-stand, grip strength | Age-normed | Function baseline & outcome |

The physiotherapist frequently administers the **PAR-Q+ (Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire)** as a pre-exercise safety screen, and uses functional tests (6-minute walk test, timed sit-to-stand, grip dynamometry) that double as screening and outcome measures.

### 5. Designing an Education Programme for Lifestyle Diseases

An effective programme is **theory-based, tailored, multi-component and multi-level**. Key design elements:

1. **Needs assessment** (PRECEDE) — epidemiology, risk-factor prevalence, community priorities, health literacy.
2. **Clear objectives** — SMART, mapped to knowledge, attitude, behaviour and clinical outcomes.
3. **Target segmentation** — gender-, age- and literacy-appropriate messaging.
4. **Message and method** — the physiotherapist emphasises the **exercise prescription (FITT-VP: frequency, intensity, time, type, volume, progression)**, healthy diet linkage, tobacco/alcohol brief advice, and stress/sleep hygiene.
5. **Delivery channels** — group education, one-to-one counselling, community camps, mass media, mHealth/tele-education, schools and workplaces.
6. **Behaviour-change techniques** — goal-setting, self-monitoring (step counts), action planning, social support, motivational interviewing.
7. **Evaluation** — process (reach, dose), impact (knowledge, behaviour, risk factors) and outcome (incidence, function) indicators, using **RE-AIM**.

*Figure: A "screen-and-educate" pathway can be visualised as: community mobilisation → opportunistic/organised screening (BP, BMI, glucose, IDRS, PAR-Q+) → risk stratification → tailored education + exercise prescription → referral of positives for diagnosis → follow-up and re-screening, with a feedback loop for programme audit.*

### 6. Gender Considerations

Lifestyle-disease risk and presentation are gendered. Women more often have "atypical" CVD presentations and are under-screened and under-treated for heart disease; polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), gestational diabetes and menopause add female-specific cardiometabolic risk. Men have higher rates of tobacco/alcohol use and poorer health-seeking behaviour, warranting proactive outreach. Gender norms shape physical-activity access (women's limited leisure time and safe-space constraints in India). Transgender persons face additional cardiometabolic risks (some linked to hormone therapy) alongside major barriers to screening. Gender-responsive programmes therefore differentiate outreach, messaging and access.

### 7. The Physiotherapist's Role

Physiotherapists contribute across the continuum: administering pre-exercise and functional screening, delivering **structured, individualised exercise prescription** as a primary NCD intervention (the strongest single lifestyle lever the physiotherapist controls), providing behaviour-change counselling, running community and workplace education camps, and integrating into cardiac, pulmonary and metabolic rehabilitation for secondary/tertiary prevention.

## Qc. Discuss pelvic floor dysfunction and its physiotherapy management across the lifespan. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

The **pelvic floor** is a multilayered musculofascial sling suspended across the bony pelvic outlet, comprising the **levator ani group (pubococcygeus, puborectalis, iliococcygeus)** and **coccygeus**, together forming the **pelvic diaphragm**, plus the deeper endopelvic fascia and superficial urogenital muscles. Its functions are summarised by the mnemonic **"5 S's": Support** (of the pelvic viscera — bladder, uterus/prostate, rectum), **Sphincteric** control (continence of urine and faeces), **Sexual** function, **Stability** (as part of the core, with the diaphragm, transversus abdominis and multifidus), and **Sump-pump** (lymphatic/venous return).

**Pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD)** is an umbrella term for conditions arising from impaired pelvic-floor muscle (PFM) function — either **underactivity/weakness** (hypotonic: stress urinary incontinence, pelvic organ prolapse, faecal incontinence) or **overactivity/non-relaxing** (hypertonic: chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia, vaginismus, obstructed defecation, non-relaxing puborectalis) — as well as **incoordination**. The **International Continence Society (ICS)** and the International Urogynecological Association (IUGA) provide standardised terminology for these conditions. PFD affects all genders and every life stage; **pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT)**, popularised by **Arnold Kegel (1948)**, is the evidence-based first-line physiotherapy.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **Integral Theory (Petros & Ulmsten):** pelvic organ support and continence depend on the integrity of ligaments, fascia and muscles acting as a suspensory system; laxity at defined "zones" produces predictable symptoms.
- **DeLancey's "three levels of support":** Level I (apical — uterosacral/cardinal ligaments), Level II (lateral — arcus tendineus/paravaginal), Level III (distal — perineal body/urogenital diaphragm). Guides understanding of prolapse.
- **Pressure/continence mechanisms:** the **guarding reflex** and the pelvic-floor's role in maintaining a positive urethral closure pressure; the **"knack"** (a well-timed PFM contraction before a cough) counteracts stress incontinence.
- **Motor learning & core stability:** the PFM co-activate with transversus abdominis and the diaphragm; retraining uses motor-learning principles (awareness → isolation → coordination → functional integration).
- **Biopsychosocial model / ICF:** especially for chronic pelvic pain, where central sensitisation, psychological and social factors are central.
- **Life-course perspective:** PFD risk and type shift with hormonal status, obstetric events, and ageing.

### 3. Aetiology and Risk Factors Across the Lifespan

| Life stage | Predominant PFD risks | Key contributors |
|------------|----------------------|------------------|
| Childhood/adolescence | Voiding dysfunction, nocturnal enuresis, dysfunctional voiding | Behavioural, constipation, rarely neurological |
| Reproductive/pregnancy | Stress incontinence, pelvic girdle pain, diastasis recti | Pregnancy hormones (relaxin), growing load, delivery |
| Postpartum | SUI, prolapse, faecal incontinence, perineal trauma, dyspareunia | Vaginal delivery, instrumental birth, high birth weight, prolonged 2nd stage, OASIS (obstetric anal sphincter injury) |
| Midlife/menopause | Worsening SUI, urgency, prolapse, GSM (genitourinary syndrome of menopause) | Oestrogen decline, cumulative parity, ageing collagen |
| Older age (women & men) | Mixed/urge incontinence, prolapse, faecal incontinence | Ageing, comorbidity, immobility |
| Men (any age) | Post-prostatectomy incontinence, chronic pelvic pain syndrome, erectile dysfunction | Prostate surgery, pelvic pain, sport (cycling) |

General risk factors span **modifiable** (obesity, chronic cough, chronic constipation and straining, heavy lifting, high-impact exercise, smoking) and **non-modifiable** (parity, mode of delivery, ageing, connective-tissue disorders, genetics).

### 4. Assessment

Comprehensive assessment includes: **subjective history** (symptom mapping, obstetric/surgical history, bladder/bowel diary, validated questionnaires — ICIQ-UI SF, PFDI-20, PFIQ-7), **general examination**, and **specialised PFM assessment**. PFM function is graded with the **Modified Oxford Scale (0–5)** by vaginal/rectal digital palpation, and characterised using the **PERFECT scheme (Power, Endurance, Repetitions, Fast contractions, Every Contraction Timed)** described by Laycock. Adjuncts include **perineometry (manometry)**, **surface EMG biofeedback**, **real-time ultrasound (transabdominal/transperineal)**, and, for prolapse, the **POP-Q** staging system. Red-flag screening (haematuria, neurological signs, pain features) triggers referral.

### 5. Physiotherapy Management Across the Lifespan

#### 5.1 Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) — the evidence-based core

**PFMT is the recommended first-line treatment for stress and mixed urinary incontinence in women** (high-quality Cochrane evidence: women doing supervised PFMT are markedly more likely to report cure or improvement than controls). Principles:

- **Correct isolation first** — verify a genuine "lift and squeeze" (not a Valsalva or gluteal/adductor substitution), confirmed by palpation, biofeedback or ultrasound.
- **Progressive overload** — combine **maximal (strength) contractions** (sustained 6–8 s holds) with **endurance** and **fast/phasic** contractions, typically **~8–12 contractions × 3 sets/day** for a minimum of **3 months**, individualised via PERFECT findings.
- **Functional integration** — teach "**the knack**" (pre-contraction before cough/lift) and incorporate into daily activities.
- **Supervised > unsupervised** — supervised programmes with regular review outperform simple leaflet instruction.

#### 5.2 Adjunctive modalities

**Biofeedback** (EMG or manometric) aids awareness and adherence and is valuable when isolation is poor. **Electrical stimulation** and **vaginal cones/weights** assist very weak muscles. For **overactive/hypertonic PFD and chronic pelvic pain**, treatment shifts to **down-training** — diaphragmatic breathing, PFM relaxation, manual/myofascial release, trigger-point work, dilators for vaginismus, and central-sensitisation-informed pain education (never strengthening a non-relaxing floor). **Bladder training** and urge-suppression techniques address overactive bladder. **Lifestyle advice** (weight loss, fluid/caffeine modification, constipation management, defecation dynamics) underpins all care.

#### 5.3 Life-stage-specific application

- **Adolescents/children:** behavioural voiding programmes, constipation management, biofeedback for dysfunctional voiding.
- **Antenatal/postnatal:** antenatal PFMT reduces and treats incontinence and is protective postpartum; postpartum PFMT plus perineal care (see Qd).
- **Menopause:** PFMT combined with topical oestrogen (prescribed) for GSM; prolapse management.
- **Men:** **pre- and post-prostatectomy PFMT** hastens recovery of continence; PFM down-training and relaxation for chronic pelvic pain syndrome.
- **Older adults:** PFMT remains effective; combine with mobility, toileting programmes and environmental adaptation.

#### 5.4 When to refer

Prolapse beyond conservative benefit, refractory incontinence, red flags, and complex pain warrant referral to urogynaecology/urology/colorectal teams; physiotherapy remains valuable pre- and post-operatively (e.g., before/after prolapse or sphincter surgery).

<div class="callout callout-india">

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

Pelvic floor dysfunction is highly prevalent yet profoundly under-reported in India owing to stigma, the perception that leakage or prolapse is a "normal" consequence of childbearing and ageing, low awareness, and a shortage of trained women's-health physiotherapists. **Uterovaginal prolapse** is a major cause of morbidity among rural, multiparous women, linked to high parity, home deliveries, early return to heavy manual labour postpartum, and inadequate obstetric care. Maternal-health schemes — **Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY)** (cash incentive for institutional delivery) and **Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK)** (free delivery, including caesarean, and postnatal care) — have raised institutional-delivery rates, which reduces some obstetric trauma, and the **LaQshya** initiative improves labour-room and delivery quality; better perineal management at institutional births is an opportunity to prevent PFD. However, structured antenatal/postnatal pelvic-floor physiotherapy is rarely available in the public system. Cultural taboos around vaginal examination make external assessment and non-invasive biofeedback especially valuable, and female physiotherapists and privacy are essential for acceptability. Awareness-building through ASHAs and antenatal clinics, and integrating PFMT education into JSSK postnatal contacts and Ayushman Arogya Mandir services, represent practical entry points. The **NCAHP Act 2021** and growing post-graduate women's-health physiotherapy specialisation are slowly expanding capacity.
</div>

<div class="callout callout-advance">

**🔬 Recent Advances (2020–2026)**

- **Updated Cochrane reviews** continue to confirm PFMT as first-line for female stress/mixed urinary incontinence and support its role in prolapse symptom reduction and post-prostatectomy recovery.
- **Tele-rehabilitation and app-based pelvic health**: validated smartphone apps (with or without intravaginal biofeedback devices, e.g., app-connected pressure sensors) deliver guided PFMT with efficacy approaching supervised care, expanding reach where specialists are scarce — highly relevant to India.
- **Advanced biofeedback and imaging**: real-time transperineal ultrasound and app-linked EMG improve teaching of correct contraction and adherence.
- **NICE (NG123, updated) and international consensus** reaffirm supervised PFMT (≥3 months) as first-line and now recommend antenatal PFMT for prevention.
- Growing evidence for physiotherapy in **chronic pelvic pain** using down-training, manual therapy and pain neuroscience education rather than strengthening.
- Emerging use of **wearable and gamified pelvic-floor trainers** to improve engagement and adherence.
</div>

### Exam Key Points (rapid revision)

- Pelvic floor = levator ani (pubococcygeus, puborectalis, iliococcygeus) + coccygeus; functions "5 S's" — Support, Sphincteric, Sexual, Stability, Sump-pump.
- PFD = **underactive** (weakness → SUI, prolapse, faecal incontinence) or **overactive/non-relaxing** (→ chronic pelvic pain, vaginismus, obstructed defecation) or incoordination.
- Frameworks: Integral Theory (Petros), DeLancey 3 levels of support, the "knack," core co-activation, ICF/biopsychosocial for pain.
- Assessment: history + bladder diary + questionnaires (ICIQ, PFDI-20), **Modified Oxford Scale**, **PERFECT scheme**, perineometry, EMG/US biofeedback, **POP-Q** for prolapse.
- **PFMT is first-line for SUI/mixed UI (Cochrane)** — isolate correctly, ~8–12 contractions × 3 sets/day, ≥3 months, supervised > unsupervised; add biofeedback, e-stim, cones for weak muscles.
- **Overactive floor → down-training** (relaxation, breathing, manual therapy, pain education) — never strengthen.
- Life-course: children (voiding/constipation), antenatal/postnatal PFMT, menopause (+topical oestrogen), men (pre/post-prostatectomy, CPPS), elderly (PFMT + toileting).
- India: high prolapse burden, under-reporting/stigma; leverage JSY/JSSK/LaQshya and ASHAs; tele-PFMT is promising.
