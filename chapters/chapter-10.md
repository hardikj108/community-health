# Chapter 10 — Oncology and Palliative Care

*MPT Community Physiotherapy · Long-Answer Model Notes*

Cancer has moved from being an almost universally fatal diagnosis to a spectrum of conditions in which a growing proportion of people live for years or decades after treatment. The Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN 2022) estimated approximately **20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer deaths worldwide**, with the number of people alive within five years of diagnosis (the 5-year prevalence) exceeding **53 million**. India recorded an estimated **1.4–1.5 million new cases and around 9 lakh deaths** in the same period, a figure projected to rise steeply through the 2030s because of population ageing, tobacco use, and lifestyle transition. This epidemiological shift creates an enormous and largely unmet rehabilitation need: the physical, functional, and participation consequences of the tumour itself and of surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and immunotherapy accumulate across the **prehabilitation → active-treatment → survivorship → advanced-disease/end-of-life continuum**. For the community physiotherapist, oncology is therefore not a narrow hospital sub-speciality but a longitudinal, whole-population concern in which movement, exercise, symptom control, and dignity are central instruments of care.

This chapter provides postgraduate physiotherapists with an exam-ready, evidence-based treatment of community oncology and palliative-care physiotherapy, anchored throughout in the **WHO definition and principles of palliative care**, the **American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) 2019 International Multidisciplinary Roundtable on Exercise and Cancer**, the **Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC)** guidance, Cochrane systematic reviews on exercise-oncology, cancer-related fatigue and lymphoedema, the **"Exercise is Medicine in Oncology" (EIMO)** initiative, and the **International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)**. It answers four 20-mark long-answer questions:

- **Qa.** Evidence Summary of the Benefits of Physiotherapy Interventions in Cancer Care
- **Qb.** Integrating Physical Activity and Exercise as an Intervention Strategy Across the Spectrum of Cancer Care
- **Qc.** Implementing Evidence-Based Physiotherapy Interventions in Cancer Rehabilitation
- **Qd.** Physiotherapy Service Provisions in Palliative Care

## Qa. Critically summarise the evidence for the benefits of physiotherapy interventions in cancer care. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

Cancer and its treatment produce a cluster of impairments — deconditioning, cancer-related fatigue, pain, lymphoedema, peripheral neuropathy, reduced cardiorespiratory fitness, loss of muscle mass, restricted range of motion, psychological distress, and diminished quality of life (QoL) — that fall squarely within the scope of physiotherapy. **Cancer rehabilitation** is defined by Dietz's classic framework and by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a process that helps a person with cancer to obtain and maintain maximal physical, social, psychological, and vocational functioning within the limits imposed by the disease and its treatment. **Physiotherapy in oncology** is the application of movement, exercise, manual and physical-agent techniques, education, and self-management support to prevent, reduce, or reverse these impairments across the disease continuum.

Several definitions anchor this answer:

- **Cancer-related fatigue (CRF)** — the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) defines it as "a distressing, persistent, subjective sense of physical, emotional and/or cognitive tiredness or exhaustion related to cancer or cancer treatment that is not proportional to recent activity and interferes with usual functioning."
- **Cancer-related lymphoedema** — an abnormal accumulation of protein-rich interstitial fluid, most often secondary to lymph-node dissection or radiotherapy, staged by the International Society of Lymphology (ISL 0–III).
- **Prehabilitation** — the Macmillan/Royal College of Anaesthetists definition: a continuum of care from diagnosis to the start of treatment that uses assessment and interventions (exercise, nutrition, psychological support) to improve baseline function and resilience before a physiological stressor.
- **Survivorship** — from the point of diagnosis and for the balance of life (National Cancer Institute), encompassing living with, through, and beyond cancer.

The organising thesis of this answer is that a large and maturing body of randomised controlled trials (RCTs), Cochrane and other systematic reviews, and consensus guidelines now demonstrates **moderate- to high-certainty benefit of physiotherapy — especially structured exercise — on fatigue, physical function, aerobic and muscular fitness, lymphoedema control, and quality of life**, with emerging evidence for effects on treatment tolerance and, in some cancers, disease outcomes.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

Three frameworks structure the evidence:

**(a) The ICF model.** Cancer effects are mapped as *body structure/function* impairments (reduced VO₂peak, sarcopenia, lymphoedema, neuropathy), *activity* limitations (walking, stair-climbing, lifting), and *participation* restrictions (return to work, social roles), all modulated by *environmental* (access, oncology-team referral) and *personal* (age, comorbidity, motivation) factors. Physiotherapy evidence is strongest where outcomes are measured at the function and participation levels, which is where the ICF directs attention.

**(b) The "Exercise is Medicine in Oncology" (EIMO) paradigm.** Championed by ACSM and the American Cancer Society, EIMO reframes exercise from an optional adjunct to a prescribable, dose-defined therapy. The 2019 ACSM International Multidisciplinary Roundtable synthesised the evidence into specific FITT (Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type) prescriptions for defined outcomes.

**(c) Biological mechanisms.** Benefits are plausibly mediated by improved cardiorespiratory and skeletal-muscle function, reduced systemic inflammation (lower IL-6, CRP, TNF-α), improved insulin sensitivity and sex-hormone profiles, enhanced immune surveillance (NK-cell mobilisation), improved tumour perfusion/oxygenation potentially enhancing therapy delivery, and central/psychological effects on mood and self-efficacy. This mechanistic coherence strengthens causal inference from the trial data.

*Figure: A three-tier evidence pyramid — base of physiological RCTs and mechanistic studies, middle of Cochrane/systematic reviews and meta-analyses, apex of consensus guidelines (ACSM 2019, MASCC, ASCO, ESMO) — showing how single-outcome trials aggregate into practice recommendations.*

### 3. Evidence for Exercise on Cancer-Related Fatigue

CRF is the most prevalent and disabling symptom in oncology, affecting up to 80–90% of patients during treatment and persisting in a substantial minority of survivors. Paradoxically, the intervention with the strongest evidence base for CRF is **physical exercise**, not rest.

The landmark **Cochrane review by Cramp & Byron-Daniel (2012, updated evidence base)** pooled 56 RCTs and found that aerobic exercise significantly reduced fatigue during and after cancer treatment (standardised mean difference favouring exercise), with the clearest effects in breast and prostate cancer. Subsequent large meta-analyses, including the comparative-effectiveness network meta-analysis by **Mustian et al. (2017, JAMA Oncology)** of 113 unique studies (11,525 participants), concluded that **exercise and psychological interventions were superior to pharmaceutical options** (including psychostimulants) for reducing CRF, with exercise producing an effect size around 0.30 and combined exercise-plus-psychological approaches performing best.

The 2019 ACSM Roundtable graded the evidence for exercise reducing fatigue during and after treatment as **strong (Grade A/moderate-to-strong)**, recommending combined aerobic plus resistance training, approximately 30 minutes of moderate aerobic exercise three times per week and resistance training twice weekly.

### 4. Evidence for Exercise on Physical Function, Fitness and Body Composition

Cancer treatment causes measurable declines in cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂peak may fall 20–30% during chemotherapy) and skeletal-muscle mass. Systematic reviews consistently show that supervised aerobic and resistance training **improve VO₂peak, muscular strength, walking distance (6-minute walk test), and lean body mass** relative to usual care. The ACSM Roundtable rated the evidence for exercise improving physical function and health-related quality of life as **strong**, and for aerobic fitness and muscular strength as moderate-to-strong. In prostate cancer patients on androgen-deprivation therapy — a group at high risk of sarcopenia, osteoporosis, and metabolic syndrome — resistance training preserves muscle mass and bone health and counters treatment-induced adverse effects.

### 5. Evidence for Physiotherapy on Lymphoedema

Breast-cancer-related lymphoedema (BCRL) affects roughly one in five women after axillary treatment. **Complete Decongestive Therapy (CDT)** — comprising manual lymphatic drainage (MLD), multilayer short-stretch compression bandaging, remedial exercise, and skin care — is the internationally accepted standard, endorsed by the International Society of Lymphology. Evidence shows CDT and compression reliably reduce limb volume. Two important nuances have emerged:

- A **Cochrane review (Ezzo et al., 2015)** on MLD found that MLD is safe and may offer additional benefit when added to compression, particularly in mild-to-moderate lymphoedema, though compression is the core effective element.
- The **PAL trial (Schmitz et al., NEJM 2009)** overturned decades of dogma by showing that **slowly progressive resistance training did not worsen and may protect against lymphoedema**, dismantling the old advice to avoid upper-limb exercise after breast-cancer surgery.

Early physiotherapy after axillary dissection also reduces the incidence of lymphoedema and shoulder dysfunction, and early detection using bioimpedance spectroscopy with prompt compression can reverse subclinical lymphoedema.

### 6. Evidence on Pain, Neuropathy, Bone Health and Surgical Recovery

Physiotherapy contributes to management of **chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN)** through balance and sensorimotor training, which reviews suggest improves balance and reduces falls, and possibly symptom severity. For **aromatase-inhibitor-associated arthralgia**, exercise reduces joint pain and improves adherence to endocrine therapy (the HOPE trial, Irwin et al.). Weight-bearing and resistance exercise support **bone health** in patients rendered osteopenic by hormone therapy. After thoracic and abdominal cancer surgery, **respiratory physiotherapy and early mobilisation** reduce postoperative pulmonary complications and length of stay; after head-and-neck cancer, jaw and neck rehabilitation reduces trismus and dysfunction.

### 7. Evidence on Quality of Life, Psychological Outcomes and Survival

Across tumour types, exercise interventions produce **small-to-moderate improvements in global quality of life, anxiety, depression, and self-esteem**, as confirmed in Cochrane reviews (Mishra et al., 2012) of exercise for QoL in cancer survivors. Most provocative is the **observational and emerging trial evidence linking physical activity to reduced cancer-specific and all-cause mortality** in breast, colorectal, and prostate cancer (relative risk reductions of roughly 30–40% in cohort studies). The **CHALLENGE trial (Courneya et al., 2025, NEJM)** — a structured exercise programme after colon-cancer chemotherapy — provided landmark RCT-level evidence that exercise improves disease-free survival, moving exercise from supportive care toward a disease-modifying intervention.

### 8. Limitations of the Evidence Base

A balanced critique is expected at postgraduate level. Much of the literature concentrates on **breast and prostate cancer and on relatively fit, younger, self-selected participants**; frail, elderly, advanced-disease, and low-resource populations are under-represented. Trials vary in exercise dose, supervision, and control-group activity, producing heterogeneity. Blinding is impossible, risking performance/detection bias, and long-term adherence data are limited. Nonetheless, the **consistency of direction across hundreds of trials and multiple tumour types**, biological plausibility, and dose-response relationships justify the strong guideline recommendations.

## Qb. Discuss the integration of physical activity and exercise as an intervention strategy across the spectrum of cancer care — from prehabilitation through treatment and survivorship to end of life. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

The traditional model treated exercise as an optional add-on delivered, if at all, only after treatment was complete. Contemporary exercise-oncology reframes physical activity as a **continuous, phase-adapted intervention threaded through the entire cancer journey**. This continuum is often described as **prehabilitation → active treatment (rehabilitation) → survivorship → advanced disease and end of life**, each phase having distinct goals, dosing, and safety considerations but sharing a single organising principle: preserve or restore movement capacity and participation.

Key definitions:

- **Physical activity** — any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscle that expends energy (WHO). **Exercise** is planned, structured, repetitive physical activity aimed at improving/maintaining fitness.
- **Prehabilitation** — interventions from diagnosis to the start of treatment to optimise physiological and psychological reserve before a stressor (surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy).
- **Rehabilitation** — restorative interventions during and after active treatment to reduce impairment and disability.
- **Survivorship care** — long-term management of late/long-term effects, secondary prevention, and health promotion after primary treatment.
- **End-of-life / palliative exercise** — activity aimed at comfort, function, autonomy, and dignity rather than fitness gains.

The **WHO global recommendation** — at least 150–300 minutes of moderate or 75–150 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity weekly, plus muscle-strengthening on two or more days — provides the population baseline, which the ACSM 2019 Roundtable then tailors to specific cancer outcomes.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

**(a) The "Exercise is Medicine in Oncology" continuum.** EIMO conceptualises exercise as a prescribable therapy with a specific FITT dose for each target outcome and each treatment phase — analogous to titrating a drug.

**(b) The physiological-reserve / "surgical fitness" model (prehabilitation).** Major cancer treatment imposes a physiological insult; a patient's ability to recover depends on baseline reserve. Prehabilitation aims to raise the pre-treatment fitness "ceiling" so that the treatment-induced decline leaves the patient above the threshold of disability — the basis of the widely cited prehabilitation curve.

**(c) The ICF and participation.** Across all phases, the goal is not merely improved VO₂peak but restored **activity and participation** — returning to work, family roles, and community life — with environmental and personal factors shaping feasibility.

**(d) Behaviour-change models.** Sustaining activity across years of survivorship requires theory-driven support (Social Cognitive Theory, the Transtheoretical Model, and the COM-B/Behaviour Change Wheel) to build self-efficacy, habit, and social support.

*Figure: The prehabilitation curve — two trajectories of functional capacity over time. Without prehab, function starts lower, dips sharply after treatment and recovers slowly, sometimes never returning to baseline. With prehab, the starting point is raised, the post-treatment nadir is shallower, and recovery is faster and more complete.*

### 3. Phase 1 — Prehabilitation (Diagnosis to Start of Treatment)

Prehabilitation exploits the "**teachable moment**" between diagnosis and treatment. **Multimodal prehabilitation** combines exercise (aerobic and resistance training, often with inspiratory-muscle training before thoracic surgery), nutritional optimisation (protein, correction of malnutrition), and psychological support (anxiety reduction, smoking cessation).

Evidence is strongest in **colorectal, lung, and upper-gastrointestinal surgery**, where prehabilitation improves preoperative functional capacity (6MWT), reduces postoperative pulmonary and overall complications, and shortens length of stay. Prehabilitation before neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy can attenuate the fitness decline these treatments cause. Programmes are typically short (2–6 weeks, dictated by the treatment timeline), high-frequency, and where possible supervised or tele-supervised. Baseline assessment — cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) where available, or field tests such as the 6MWT and sit-to-stand — both risk-stratifies and sets the exercise dose.

### 4. Phase 2 — Exercise During Active Treatment

During chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine, or immunotherapy, the goals shift from building reserve to **maintaining function, countering fatigue, preserving muscle and bone, and supporting treatment tolerance and completion**. The ACSM prescription for this phase is broadly:

- **Aerobic**: moderate intensity, ~3 sessions/week, ~30 minutes, adjusted to daily symptoms.
- **Resistance**: 2–3 sessions/week, major muscle groups, 2 sets of 8–15 repetitions, progressed slowly.
- **Flexibility/mobility**: as needed to preserve range (e.g., shoulder after breast surgery).

Concurrent exercise reduces fatigue, maintains cardiorespiratory fitness and lean mass, and in several trials improves chemotherapy relative-dose-intensity and completion rates. **Safety modifications** are essential: avoid public gyms and swimming pools during neutropenia; caution with resistance loading in thrombocytopenia or bone metastases; monitor for anaemia, cardiotoxicity (anthracyclines, trastuzumab), and treatment-day symptoms. Exercise is titrated — not abandoned — around nadir counts and symptom flares.

### 5. Phase 3 — Survivorship

Most people completing curative treatment enter a survivorship phase that may last decades and carries risks of **late effects (cardiotoxicity, second cancers, osteoporosis), long-term fatigue, weight gain, metabolic syndrome, and psychological distress**. Here exercise serves both **rehabilitation** (resolving residual impairments) and **secondary/tertiary prevention**.

The evidence supports meeting WHO/ACSM targets: 150+ minutes/week moderate aerobic activity plus twice-weekly resistance training, individualised to residual impairments (e.g., lymphoedema-safe upper-limb progression, balance training for CIPN). Physical activity in survivorship is associated with reduced recurrence and mortality in breast, colorectal, and prostate cancer, improved QoL, and reduced fatigue and depression. The community physiotherapist's role expands to **behaviour-change support, community exercise referral, return-to-work rehabilitation, and integration with primary care** — the domain most suited to Health & Wellness Centres and tele-rehabilitation.

### 6. Phase 4 — Advanced Disease and End of Life

Exercise remains valuable even in advanced and palliative settings, though goals change from fitness to **maintaining independence, mobility, comfort, and dignity, and reducing symptom burden**. Systematic reviews (including Cochrane work on exercise in advanced/incurable cancer) show that individualised, low-to-moderate activity is feasible and can improve fatigue, QoL, and physical function without harm in selected patients. Prescriptions are gentle, flexible, and frequently reassessed — chair-based exercise, functional mobility practice, breathing techniques, energy-conservation, and transfers training — delivered within a palliative-care framework and stopped or adapted as the patient deteriorates. The emphasis on **autonomy and staying mobile as long as possible** aligns exercise with the WHO palliative-care goal of quality of life.

The table synthesises the continuum.

| Phase | Primary goals | Typical exercise dose | Key safety issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prehabilitation | Raise reserve before treatment | 2–6 wk multimodal aerobic + resistance + IMT | Time-limited window; comorbidity screening |
| Active treatment | Maintain function, reduce fatigue, aid tolerance | Aerobic 3×/wk 30 min + resistance 2–3×/wk, symptom-adjusted | Neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, cardiotoxicity, bone mets |
| Survivorship | Reverse impairment; prevent recurrence/late effects | Meet WHO targets: 150 min aerobic + 2× resistance | Lymphoedema progression, CIPN falls, deconditioning |
| Advanced / end of life | Comfort, mobility, dignity, symptom relief | Gentle, individualised, frequently reassessed | Fatigue, bone fragility, rapid change; goals of care |

### 7. Delivery Models and Referral Pathways

Integration requires embedding exercise into oncology pathways: **automatic referral/screening** at diagnosis and treatment milestones (the "prospective surveillance model"), triage by risk (supervised specialist rehab vs community/self-managed programmes), and clear communication between oncology and physiotherapy. Delivery ranges from hospital-based supervised programmes to community exercise-referral schemes, home programmes, and tele-rehabilitation, allowing a stepped-care approach matched to complexity and resources.

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

In India the exercise-across-the-continuum model is nascent but promising. Late-stage presentation means many patients enter the pathway already deconditioned, heightening the value of both prehabilitation and palliative exercise. The **National Cancer Grid** and Tata Memorial Centre have begun incorporating supportive care and rehabilitation into resource-stratified guidelines, and prehabilitation programmes have been piloted before major oncological surgery in tertiary centres. **Physical-activity promotion** aligns with the population strategy of **NP-NCD** and with the **Fit India Movement** and **Eat Right India**, which the community physiotherapist can leverage for survivorship. **Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (Health & Wellness Centres)** — mandated to deliver NCD screening and yoga/physical-activity sessions — are the natural community platform for delivering survivorship exercise and follow-up close to home, especially in rural districts underserved by tertiary rehab. **Yoga**, with a growing (though heterogeneous) evidence base for fatigue and QoL and strong cultural acceptability, is a pragmatic component supported by AYUSH infrastructure. Barriers include workforce shortage, lack of structured survivorship pathways, out-of-pocket cost, and low awareness that exercise during treatment is safe and beneficial. Tele-rehabilitation via the **Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM)** and eSanjeevani telemedicine platform offers a scalable route to extend supervised exercise into homes.

## Qc. Describe how evidence-based physiotherapy interventions can be implemented in cancer rehabilitation. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

Efficacy demonstrated in trials does not automatically translate into routine practice — the well-documented **"evidence-to-practice gap."** Systematic reviews confirm that structured exercise and physiotherapy benefit people with cancer, yet the majority of patients are never assessed, referred, or prescribed exercise. **Implementation** is the science and practice of moving proven interventions into real-world clinical systems reliably, at scale, and sustainably.

Definitions:

- **Evidence-based practice (EBP)** — the integration of best research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values (Sackett).
- **Implementation science** — the study of methods to promote the uptake of research findings into routine practice to improve health.
- **Cancer rehabilitation** — restorative, supportive, preventive, and palliative interventions (Dietz's four categories) to maximise function and participation for people with cancer.

The thesis of this answer is that successful implementation of cancer-rehabilitation physiotherapy requires **explicit use of implementation frameworks (Knowledge-to-Action, RE-AIM, CFIR), embedded screening and referral pathways, a stratified/stepped-care service model, a trained workforce, robust outcome measurement, and adaptation to local (including Indian) resource realities.**

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

**(a) Knowledge-to-Action (KTA) cycle (Graham et al.).** KTA distinguishes *knowledge creation* (research → synthesis → tools/guidelines) from an *action cycle*: identify the problem, adapt knowledge to local context, assess barriers/facilitators, select and tailor interventions, monitor use, evaluate outcomes, and sustain. It is an ideal spine for planning a cancer-rehab service.

**(b) RE-AIM.** Evaluates implementation across **Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation (fidelity), and Maintenance** — ensuring a programme is not only effective in a trial but reaches the intended population and is sustained.

**(c) Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).** Organises determinants across five domains — intervention characteristics, inner setting (the clinic/hospital), outer setting (policy, funding), individuals (clinicians, patients), and process — to diagnose barriers.

**(d) The Prospective Surveillance Model (PSM).** A cancer-specific implementation model in which patients are assessed at baseline and re-screened at regular intervals through and beyond treatment, enabling early detection and pre-emptive management of impairments (e.g., lymphoedema, shoulder dysfunction) rather than reactive referral.

*Figure: The Knowledge-to-Action funnel feeding an embedded rehabilitation pathway — guideline synthesis at the top, local adaptation and barrier assessment in the middle, and an operational screen-triage-treat-monitor loop at the clinical coalface.*

### 3. Step 1 — Synthesise and Localise the Evidence

Implementation begins by translating global guidelines (ACSM 2019, ASCO, ESMO, MASCC, NCCN survivorship) into **local clinical pathways and standard operating procedures**. This includes defining exercise FITT prescriptions for each phase and tumour group, safety/contraindication checklists (e.g., bone-metastasis load precautions, neutropenia rules), and lymphoedema-management protocols (CDT). Resource-stratified adaptation — offering the best feasible option at each resource level — mirrors the National Cancer Grid approach and is essential in India.

### 4. Step 2 — Embed Screening and Referral (the Pathway)

The single most important implementation lever is **systematic, automatic identification** rather than reliance on ad-hoc referral. Practical mechanisms:

- **Baseline rehabilitation screening** at diagnosis (function, fatigue, fitness, lymphoedema risk, distress) using brief validated tools.
- **Prospective surveillance** re-screening at treatment milestones.
- **Embedded triggers** in the oncology workflow and electronic records that prompt physiotherapy referral.
- **Triage/stratification** to the right level of care.

### 5. Step 3 — A Stratified / Stepped-Care Service Model

Given finite specialist resources, cancer-rehab is best delivered as **stepped care** matched to complexity, as summarised below.

| Tier | Patient profile | Intervention | Setting/provider |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universal (Tier 0) | All patients | Physical-activity advice, education, self-management materials | Oncology team, HWC, digital |
| Low complexity (Tier 1) | Mild impairment, low risk | Community/home exercise, tele-rehab, group classes | Community physiotherapist, exercise referral |
| Moderate (Tier 2) | Significant impairment (fatigue, deconditioning, early lymphoedema) | Supervised individualised programme, CDT | Specialist oncology physiotherapist (outpatient) |
| High complexity (Tier 3) | Complex/advanced disease, multiple impairments | Intensive multidisciplinary rehab, palliative rehab | Tertiary cancer-rehab team |

This model channels scarce specialist time to those who most need it while extending reach through community and digital provision.

### 6. Step 4 — Workforce, Training and Multidisciplinary Teamwork

Oncology physiotherapy requires specific competencies (treatment-related precautions, lymphoedema/CDT certification, exercise prescription in the medically complex). Implementation therefore needs **training and credentialing, mentorship, and clear roles within a multidisciplinary team** (oncologist, nurse, dietitian, psychologist, occupational therapist, palliative-care team). Clinical champions and leadership support (CFIR "inner setting") are repeatedly identified as decisive facilitators.

### 7. Step 5 — Outcome Measurement, Audit and Sustainability

Implementation must be measured. A **minimum core outcome set** — e.g., fatigue (Brief Fatigue Inventory), function/performance status (ECOG, 6MWT, sit-to-stand), QoL (EORTC QLQ-C30, FACT), limb volume for lymphoedema, and patient-reported outcomes — enables audit against RE-AIM dimensions. Sustainability depends on funding (packaging rehab into reimbursement/PM-JAY), integration into routine records, and continuous quality improvement.

### 8. Barriers and Enablers

| Barriers | Enablers |
|---|---|
| No structured referral pathway; reliance on ad-hoc referral | Automatic screening / prospective surveillance |
| Shortage of trained oncology physiotherapists | Training, credentialing, task-sharing, community upskilling |
| Clinician uncertainty about exercise safety | Clear safety protocols; guideline dissemination |
| Patient fatigue, distress, low awareness, cost | Education, behaviour-change support, financial protection |
| Geographic access, especially rural | Tele-rehabilitation, community & HWC delivery |
| Funding not linked to rehab | Reimbursement packages, policy integration |

### 9. Digital and Tele-Rehabilitation as an Implementation Accelerator

Tele-rehabilitation directly addresses the reach and access barriers that most constrain cancer rehab. Remotely supervised exercise (video, apps, wearables, remote monitoring) extends specialist prescription into homes and rural areas, supports adherence, and proved feasible and effective during and after the COVID-19 pandemic — a powerful, scalable implementation vehicle when integrated with in-person triage for high-complexity patients.

<div class="callout callout-india">

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

Implementing cancer-rehab physiotherapy in India means grappling with a **workforce and access gap**: dedicated oncology-physiotherapy services and structured survivorship pathways exist mainly in tertiary centres (Tata Memorial Mumbai, AIIMS, RCC Thiruvananthapuram, Kidwai Bengaluru, Adyar Chennai), while most patients are rural and present late. A **resource-stratified, stepped-care model** — mirroring the **National Cancer Grid**'s tiered guidelines — is therefore the pragmatic implementation strategy: specialist rehab at State Cancer Institutes/Tertiary Care Cancer Centres (created under the **Strengthening of Tertiary Care for Cancer** scheme), and universal/low-complexity care pushed out to district hospitals and **Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (Health & Wellness Centres)** through **task-sharing and community-physiotherapist upskilling**. Financial protection via **Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY** and the **NP-NCD** platform provide policy scaffolding, while the **Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM)** and **eSanjeevani** telemedicine network offer a route to tele-rehabilitation across distances. The **NCAHP Act 2021** and **RCI** frameworks support standardising physiotherapy competencies. Key implementation actions for India: create referral triggers within existing NCD/cancer clinics, develop short oncology-rehab training modules, embed lymphoedema (CDT) and exercise protocols into National Cancer Grid pathways, and use community health workers (ASHAs) for follow-up and adherence support.

**🔬 Recent Advances (2020–2026)**

- Growth of **implementation-science trials in exercise-oncology**, applying RE-AIM and CFIR to embed exercise referral into routine oncology (e.g., automatic referral and "exercise as the fourth pillar of care" models).
- **Tele-rehabilitation and hybrid (in-person + remote) models** scaled rapidly post-COVID, improving reach and being retained as standard delivery options.
- **Prospective surveillance model** adoption for lymphoedema and shoulder morbidity, aided by bioimpedance surveillance.
- Development of **core outcome sets and PROM dashboards** integrated into electronic records to standardise measurement.
- **AI-assisted risk stratification** and wearable-derived activity monitoring emerging to triage patients to appropriate rehab tiers.
- International guideline updates (ASCO exercise/diet/weight 2022; MASCC supportive-care standards) providing implementable, graded recommendations.

## Qd. Discuss the role and service provisions of physiotherapy in palliative care. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

Palliative care addresses the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual suffering of people facing life-threatening illness. Physiotherapy is a core, though frequently under-recognised, component: even where cure is impossible, movement, symptom control, and preserved function are central to **quality of life and dignity** — the defining goals of palliative care.

The **World Health Organization (2002) definition** states that palliative care is "an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing the problems associated with life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual." Its **principles** include: affirming life and regarding dying as a normal process; intending neither to hasten nor postpone death; integrating physical, psychological, and spiritual care; offering a support system to help patients live as actively as possible until death; supporting the family through illness and bereavement; using a team approach; enhancing quality of life and possibly influencing the course of illness; and being applicable **early in the course of illness, alongside disease-modifying therapy**.

**Palliative rehabilitation** applies rehabilitative principles within this framework — not to reverse disease but to maximise function, comfort, autonomy, and participation across the trajectory. Dietz's category of **"palliative rehabilitation"** captures interventions that eliminate or reduce complications and provide comfort/support when disease is advanced.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

**(a) WHO public-health model of palliative care.** Access rests on four pillars: appropriate **policy**, adequate **drug availability** (notably opioids/morphine), **education** of professionals and public, and **implementation** across levels of care — a framework directly relevant to service provision.

**(b) Total pain (Cicely Saunders).** Suffering is physical, psychological, social, and spiritual; physiotherapy contributes to each dimension (pain relief, restored function/independence, social participation, and a sense of hope and control).

**(c) The ICF in palliative care.** Focus shifts toward optimising **activity and participation** and modifying environmental/personal factors even as body-function impairments progress — keeping the patient as independent and engaged as possible.

**(d) Rehabilitation in the palliative trajectory (Dietz).** Four goals shift with disease stage: preventive, restorative, supportive, and palliative — physiotherapy adapts continuously as function declines.

*Figure: A trajectory diagram showing physiotherapy goals evolving with disease progression — from restorative and supportive interventions maintaining independence, through symptom-directed and comfort-focused care, to terminal-phase positioning, breathlessness relief, and family/carer support.*

### 3. Roles of Physiotherapy in Palliative Care

**(a) Pain management.** Physiotherapy contributes non-pharmacological pain relief through positioning, TENS, gentle exercise and mobilisation, heat/cold, manual therapy, and relaxation — complementing (never replacing) analgesia within the WHO analgesic ladder.

**(b) Management of breathlessness (dyspnoea).** One of the most distressing symptoms in advanced cancer and end-stage disease. Physiotherapy offers **breathing control, positioning, pacing and energy conservation, the use of a handheld fan, airway-clearance techniques, and anxiety-management/relaxation** — an evidence-supported non-pharmacological package central to breathlessness-support services.

**(c) Fatigue and deconditioning.** Individualised gentle exercise, activity pacing, and energy-conservation strategies reduce cancer-related fatigue and maintain function even in advanced disease.

**(d) Maintaining mobility, function and safety.** Transfers, gait re-education, provision and training in walking aids and wheelchairs, and falls-risk reduction preserve independence and reduce carer burden.

**(e) Lymphoedema and oedema management.** Modified decongestive therapy, compression, gentle MLD, positioning, and skin care manage lymphoedema and dependency oedema for comfort and function.

**(f) Respiratory care and secretion management.** Positioning and airway-clearance for retained secretions; in the terminal phase, gentle repositioning for comfort and management of "death rattle" alongside the team.

**(g) Psychological support, education and carer training.** Restoring a sense of control and hope, teaching families safe handling, positioning, and pressure-area care, and supporting home care.

**(h) Terminal-phase care.** Emphasis on **comfort positioning, pressure-area care, gentle passive movements, and support of breathing and relaxation**, with goals continuously renegotiated with patient, family, and team.

### 4. Settings and Models of Service Delivery

Palliative physiotherapy is delivered across a continuum of settings, summarised below.

| Setting | Role of physiotherapy | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Tertiary/hospital palliative-care unit | Complex symptom control, rehab, MDT | Specialist team, inpatient |
| Hospice | Symptom relief, function, comfort, family support | Holistic, often NGO-run |
| Home-based / domiciliary care | Mobility, carer training, equipment, breathlessness | Community physiotherapist, home visits |
| Outpatient / day-care palliative clinic | Exercise, lymphoedema, education | Ambulatory, group programmes |
| Community / primary care | Early palliative care, follow-up, referral | HWC, ASHA-supported |

The WHO and Lancet Commission emphasise **community- and home-based palliative care** as the most equitable model in resource-limited settings, with the physiotherapist as a key home-visiting team member.

### 5. The Multidisciplinary Team and the Physiotherapist's Place

Palliative care is inherently a **team endeavour**: physician, nurse, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, social worker, counsellor/psychologist, chaplain/spiritual-care provider, pharmacist, dietitian, and trained volunteers. The physiotherapist contributes function-focused assessment and intervention, shares in goal-setting aligned to patient wishes, and supports smooth transitions of care. Clear communication and shared decision-making — respecting patient autonomy and advance-care preferences — are essential.

### 6. Principles Guiding Palliative Physiotherapy Practice

- **Patient-centred, goal-negotiated care** — goals defined by the patient's priorities, revisited as the condition changes.
- **Flexibility and frequent reassessment** — dosing and aims adapt to a fluctuating, often declining trajectory.
- **"Do no harm" and comfort-first** — avoid burdensome interventions; weigh benefit against fatigue and distress.
- **Family and carer inclusion** — training and psychological support extend the benefit and enable home care.
- **Realistic hope and dignity** — maintaining as much independence and participation as possible for as long as possible.

<div class="callout callout-india">

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

India carries a vast palliative-care need — millions require palliative care annually — yet **coverage remains under 1–2% of those in need**, among the lowest globally, and the country's opioid consumption for pain relief is a small fraction of the global mean. Landmark developments have begun to change this. The amendment of the **Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act in 2014** simplified access to **oral morphine**, designating a single Recognised Medical Institution licence and easing the earlier regulatory paralysis that had left cancer patients in untreated pain. The **National Programme for Palliative Care (NPPC), launched in 2012** under the National Health Mission, provides the policy framework for integrating palliative care from district hospitals down to community level, and palliative care features in **NP-NCD** and Ayushman Bharat's comprehensive-primary-care package delivered via **Health & Wellness Centres**. The internationally celebrated **Kerala model / Neighbourhood Network in Palliative Care (NNPC)** and **Pallium India** (founded by Dr M R Rajagopal, the "father of palliative care in India") pioneered **community-owned, volunteer-driven, home-based palliative care** — a model in which the community physiotherapist provides home mobility, breathlessness, and carer-training support at scale and at low cost. Kerala remains the only Indian state with a declared palliative-care policy and near-universal community coverage. Challenges persist nationally: workforce shortage, uneven opioid availability, limited physiotherapy inclusion in palliative teams, and rural inequity — making the community physiotherapist central to expanding equitable, home-based palliative rehabilitation.

</div>

<div class="callout callout-advance">

**🔬 Recent Advances (2020–2026)**

- The **Lancet Commission on Palliative Care and Pain Relief** reframed access to palliative care and morphine as a matter of global health equity, spurring policy attention including in India.
- Systematic reviews confirm **exercise and rehabilitation are feasible, safe, and beneficial in advanced/incurable cancer** for fatigue, function, and QoL, legitimising physiotherapy's palliative role.
- **Tele-palliative care and tele-rehabilitation** expanded after COVID-19, extending breathlessness management, carer training, and mobility support to home-bound patients via **eSanjeevani/ABDM**.
- Growth of **early integrated palliative care** (alongside oncology treatment from diagnosis), following evidence that early palliative care improves QoL and possibly survival (Temel et al.), broadening the window for palliative rehabilitation.
- Non-pharmacological **breathlessness-support / integrated breathlessness services** (breathing-thoracic-relaxation packages, handheld fan) consolidated as evidence-based physiotherapy-led interventions.
- Expansion of the **Kerala/NNPC community model** and Pallium India training programmes, informing national scale-up and NPPC operationalisation.
