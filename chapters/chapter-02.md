# Chapter 2 — Health Promotion & Health Education

*MPT Community Physiotherapy · Long-Answer Model Notes*

Health promotion and health education form the preventive backbone of community physiotherapy. Where clinical physiotherapy treats established impairment, the community physiotherapist works "upstream" — modifying the behavioural, social, environmental and policy determinants that generate non-communicable disease, disability, sedentariness, falls and musculoskeletal morbidity in the first place. The World Health Organization's **Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion (1986)** redefined health promotion as *"the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health,"* shifting the field from disease-oriented education toward empowerment, healthy public policy and supportive environments. For a postgraduate physiotherapist, mastery of health-promotion **components, strategies, planning frameworks (PRECEDE-PROCEED), behaviour-change theories (Health Belief Model, Social Cognitive Theory, Transtheoretical Model), diffusion of innovations, and the RE-AIM implementation-evaluation model** is essential to design, deliver, monitor and sustain population-level interventions across schools, workplaces, health facilities and communities.

This chapter answers nine 20-mark long-answer questions that together span the full cycle of health-promotion and health-education practice — from designing intervention components and IEC resources, through planning and settings-based implementation, to monitoring, overcoming barriers and training the frontline workforce:

- **Qa.** Components of Health Promotion Interventions
- **Qb.** Strategies for Health Promotion Interventions
- **Qc.** Implementation and Monitoring of Health Promotion Interventions in Different Settings
- **Qd.** Design and Development of IEC (Information, Education, Communication) Resources
- **Qe.** Planning for IEC Interventions
- **Qf.** Implementation and Monitoring of Health Education Interventions
- **Qg.** Strategies for Effective Implementation of Health Education Interventions
- **Qh.** Facilitators and Barriers to Implementation of Health Education Interventions
- **Qi.** Training of Healthcare Providers, Caregivers, Community Workers, and Volunteers in Health Education Delivery

## Qa. Describe the components of health promotion interventions. Discuss how a community physiotherapist can apply these components to design a population-level intervention. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Health promotion** is defined by the WHO Ottawa Charter (1986) as *"the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health."* It moves beyond a focus on individual behaviour toward a wide range of social and environmental interventions. The subsequent **Bangkok Charter for Health Promotion in a Globalized World (2005)** broadened this to *"the process of enabling people to increase control over their health and its determinants, and thereby improve their health."*

A **health promotion intervention** is a planned, theory-informed, multi-component set of activities delivered to a defined population to influence the determinants of health — behavioural, social, economic, environmental and political — in order to prevent disease, promote wellbeing and reduce health inequities. It is distinguished from **health education** (a component focused on informed voluntary behaviour change) by its wider ecological scope, embracing policy, environment and community action.

For the **community physiotherapist**, health promotion interventions are the vehicle for primary and secondary prevention of the non-communicable diseases (NCDs), musculoskeletal disorders, falls, and disability that dominate the community caseload. World Physiotherapy (WCPT) explicitly recognises health promotion and disease prevention as core roles of the profession.

The essential **components** of any health promotion intervention are the identifiable building blocks that, in combination, produce sustainable change. They can be organised along the five action areas of the Ottawa Charter and the operational elements of a planned programme.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

Three complementary frameworks structure the components of a health promotion intervention:

- **Ottawa Charter (WHO, 1986)** — five action areas: (1) build healthy public policy, (2) create supportive environments, (3) strengthen community action, (4) develop personal skills, (5) reorient health services. These are the *strategic* components.
- **PRECEDE-PROCEED model (Green & Kreuter)** — organises intervention components around **predisposing, reinforcing and enabling factors** and links them to educational, environmental and policy/regulatory/organisational strategies (the "PROCEED" phase).
- **Socio-ecological model (McLeroy et al., 1988)** — nests components across intrapersonal, interpersonal, organisational, community and public-policy levels, ensuring the intervention is *multi-level*.

The **Tannahill model** additionally frames health promotion as three overlapping spheres — **health education, prevention, and health protection** — a useful lens for identifying which components are present.

### 3. Core Component 1 — Health Education & Personal Skill Development

This is the informational and skill-building component: developing personal and social skills that enable people to make healthy choices. It includes one-to-one counselling, group education, mass-media messaging, skills demonstrations (e.g., exercise technique, ergonomic lifting, self-monitoring of blood pressure/glucose) and life-skills training. Its theoretical anchors are the **Health Belief Model** (perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, self-efficacy) and **Social Cognitive Theory** (self-efficacy, observational learning, reciprocal determinism).

For the physiotherapist, this component delivers exercise literacy, joint-protection education, back-care programmes and fall-prevention self-management.

### 4. Core Component 2 — Healthy Public Policy & Advocacy

Health-promoting policy makes the healthy choice the easy (and default) choice. Components include legislation (tobacco control, helmet/seat-belt laws), fiscal measures (sugar-sweetened-beverage taxes), regulation (food labelling, built-environment standards for walkability and accessibility) and organisational policy (workplace sitting-break policies). Advocacy — building the case to decision-makers and mobilising media and coalitions — is the mechanism through which the physiotherapist influences this component (e.g., advocating for accessible public spaces and safe cycling infrastructure).

### 5. Core Component 3 — Supportive Environments

This component modifies physical, social and economic environments so they promote rather than damage health: safe parks and walking tracks, workplace ergonomics, age-friendly and disability-friendly built environments, smoke-free zones, and school playgrounds. The community physiotherapist contributes environmental audits (accessibility, fall-hazard mapping in homes and public spaces) and recommends environmental modifications.

### 6. Core Component 4 — Community Action & Participation

Empowering communities to define their own priorities and act collectively is central. Components include community mobilisation, participatory needs assessment, capacity building of local groups (self-help groups, patient support groups, Mahila Arogya Samitis), and intersectoral partnership. This reflects the principle of **community participation** enshrined in the Alma-Ata Declaration (1978) on Primary Health Care.

### 7. Core Component 5 — Reorienting Health Services

Health services must move "beyond the clinic" toward prevention, promotion and equity. Components include integrating screening and counselling into routine care, task-shifting to community health workers, and embedding physiotherapy-led prevention (fall clinics, NCD exercise programmes) within primary care.

### 8. Operational / Cross-cutting Components

Beyond the five Ottawa areas, every well-designed intervention contains operational components:

- **Needs assessment & situational analysis** (epidemiological, behavioural, social, community diagnosis).
- **Defined target population & segmentation.**
- **SMART objectives** (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound).
- **Behaviour-change theory** driving the design.
- **IEC/BCC/SBCC communication package.**
- **Intersectoral collaboration & partnerships** (Health-in-All-Policies).
- **Resource mobilisation** (human, financial, material).
- **Monitoring & evaluation (M&E) framework** (process, impact, outcome; RE-AIM).
- **Sustainability & scale-up plan.**
- **Equity lens** (reaching the disadvantaged; proportionate universalism).

The following table maps the components to Ottawa action areas and physiotherapy examples:

| Component | Ottawa action area | PRECEDE factor addressed | Physiotherapy example |
|-----------|-------------------|--------------------------|-----------------------|
| Health education / skills | Develop personal skills | Predisposing | Group exercise class + back-care demo |
| Policy & advocacy | Build healthy public policy | Enabling / reinforcing | Advocacy for walkable neighbourhoods |
| Supportive environments | Create supportive environments | Enabling | Home fall-hazard modification |
| Community action | Strengthen community action | Reinforcing | Self-help group for arthritis |

## Qb. Discuss the strategies for health promotion interventions. Illustrate with examples relevant to community physiotherapy practice. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

A **strategy** in health promotion is a broad, planned approach — the *"how"* — through which the components of an intervention are delivered to achieve defined health objectives. Whereas *components* are the building blocks, *strategies* are the methods of action. The WHO Ottawa Charter (1986) provides the canonical set of **five health-promotion action strategies** (build healthy public policy; create supportive environments; strengthen community action; develop personal skills; reorient health services) and three **basic strategies / prerequisites** — **advocate, enable, mediate**.

For the community physiotherapist, health-promotion strategies are the tactical repertoire used to prevent NCDs, reduce sedentary behaviour, prevent falls and injuries, and manage chronic musculoskeletal conditions at population scale. Effective interventions almost always combine *multiple* strategies (a "combined" or "multi-strategy" approach), because single-strategy interventions (e.g., leaflets alone) consistently under-perform.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

Strategies can be classified along several axes:

- **By Ottawa action area** (policy, environment, community, skills, services).
- **By the three Ottawa mechanisms — Advocate, Enable, Mediate.**
- **By socio-ecological level** (individual → policy).
- **By approach:** medical/preventive, behaviour-change, educational, empowerment, and social-change approaches (Ewles & Simnett; Naidoo & Wills, *Foundations for Health Promotion*).
- **By behaviour-change theory** driving them (HBM, SCT, Transtheoretical/Stages of Change, Theory of Planned Behaviour, Diffusion of Innovations).

The **Naidoo & Wills five approaches** are a common exam framework: (1) **medical/preventive** (screening, immunisation), (2) **behaviour change** (persuading individuals), (3) **educational** (informed choice), (4) **empowerment** (client-led), (5) **social change** (policy/environment). A mature intervention blends these.

### 3. Strategy 1 — Advocacy

**Advocacy** is a combination of individual and social actions designed to gain political commitment, policy support, social acceptance and system support for a health goal. Tactics include lobbying decision-makers, media advocacy, coalition-building and using evidence and epidemiological data to make the case. *Physiotherapy example:* advocating with municipal authorities for barrier-free public spaces, cycle tracks and open gyms; advocating within a hospital for a fall-prevention policy.

### 4. Strategy 2 — Enabling / Empowerment

**Enabling** means taking action in partnership with individuals or groups to empower them, through mobilisation of human and material resources, to promote and protect their health. It emphasises equity and reducing differences in health status. Tactics: skill-building, self-help groups, community capacity development, providing resources and supportive environments. *Physiotherapy example:* training a stroke-survivor peer support group to lead their own exercise sessions.

### 5. Strategy 3 — Mediation

Health cannot be achieved by the health sector alone. **Mediation** is the process of bringing together diverse interests — government, NGOs, industry, media, communities — to pursue health. It underpins **intersectoral collaboration** and **Health-in-All-Policies (HiAP)**. *Physiotherapy example:* mediating between an education department, a school and parents to embed daily physical activity.

### 6. Strategy 4 — Health Education & Communication (IEC/BCC/SBCC)

Structured information and behaviour-change communication using interpersonal, group, mass-media and digital channels. **IEC** (Information, Education, Communication) → **BCC** (Behaviour Change Communication) → **SBCC** (Social & Behaviour Change Communication) represent an evolution from one-way information to participatory, multi-level communication. *Physiotherapy example:* an SBCC campaign combining WhatsApp exercise videos, community demonstrations and ASHA-led counselling to promote physical activity in diabetics.

### 7. Strategy 5 — Legislation, Fiscal & Regulatory Measures

Structural strategies that alter the choice architecture: taxation (tobacco, alcohol, sugar-sweetened beverages), bans and restrictions (smoke-free laws, advertising bans), mandatory standards (front-of-pack labelling, helmet laws, building accessibility codes). These "**WHO Best Buys**" are the most cost-effective population strategies.

### 8. Strategy 6 — Environmental & Settings-Based Modification

Reshaping the settings where people live, learn, work and play (schools, workplaces, hospitals, cities). The **settings-based approach** (Health Promoting Schools, Healthy Workplaces, Healthy Cities) is a WHO flagship strategy. *Physiotherapy example:* an ergonomics programme redesigning workstations plus micro-break reminders in an IT company.

### 9. Strategy 7 — Community Development & Participation

Bottom-up strategies that build on community assets, participatory needs assessment and collective action — reflecting Alma-Ata's principle of community participation. Tactics: community mobilisation, participatory rural appraisal, capacity building of local volunteers.

### 10. Strategy 8 — Screening & Early Detection (Preventive Medical)

Population and opportunistic screening for risk factors (blood pressure, blood glucose, BMI, tobacco use) and early detection of conditions, linked to referral and management. *Physiotherapy example:* community fall-risk screening (Timed Up-and-Go, gait assessment) with targeted balance programmes.

The following table compares the major strategies:

| Strategy | Ottawa mechanism | Socio-ecological level | Strength | Limitation |
|----------|-----------------|------------------------|----------|------------|
| Advocacy | Advocate | Policy | High reach, structural change | Slow, political resistance |
| Enabling/empowerment | Enable | Community/individual | Sustainable, equity-focused | Resource-intensive, time-consuming |
| Mediation | Mediate | Organisational/policy | Aligns sectors | Coordination complexity |
| IEC/BCC/SBCC | Enable | Individual → community | Scalable, flexible | Message alone rarely changes structure |
| Legislation/fiscal | Advocate | Policy | Cost-effective, population-wide | Needs political will, enforcement |
| Settings-based | Enable/mediate | Organisational | Reaches captive populations | Requires organisational buy-in |
| Community development | Enable | Community | Ownership, sustainability | Slow, needs facilitation skills |
| Screening | — | Individual | Early detection | Cost, false positives, needs referral link |

## Qc. Describe the implementation and monitoring of health promotion interventions in different settings. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Implementation** is the act of carrying out a planned intervention — translating design into delivery on the ground, with fidelity and adaptation to context. **Monitoring** is the continuous, systematic collection and review of programme data (inputs, activities, outputs, reach) to track whether implementation is proceeding as planned, enabling course correction. Together with **evaluation** (periodic assessment of outcomes and impact), they form the **M&E** cycle.

The **settings-based approach** to health promotion — a WHO flagship since the Ottawa Charter and the Sundsvall Statement (1991) — holds that health is created and lived in the settings of everyday life: **schools, workplaces, health-care facilities, homes, cities and communities**. Each setting has a distinct population, gatekeepers, culture, resources and constraints, so implementation and monitoring must be tailored to each. The community physiotherapist implements exercise, ergonomics, fall-prevention and NCD-risk programmes across these settings.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **RE-AIM** (Glasgow, Vogt & Boles, 1999) is the dominant framework for implementing and evaluating in real-world settings: **Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance.** It balances internal validity with external validity (translatability).
- **CFIR (Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research)** and **PRISM (Practical Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model)** analyse contextual determinants of implementation.
- **Diffusion of Innovations (Rogers)** explains how a programme spreads through a setting (innovators → early adopters → early/late majority → laggards) and the innovation attributes (relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, observability) that speed adoption.
- **Logic model / results chain** (inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact) structures the monitoring indicators.

### 3. General Implementation Process (across all settings)

1. **Situational analysis & stakeholder mapping** in the setting.
2. **Securing buy-in** from gatekeepers (head-teacher, HR manager, medical superintendent, gram panchayat).
3. **Detailed operational plan** (activities, timeline/Gantt chart, roles, budget).
4. **Capacity building** — training deliverers.
5. **Pilot / phased roll-out** to test feasibility.
6. **Full implementation** with fidelity checks.
7. **Continuous monitoring** and iterative adaptation.
8. **Institutionalisation / sustainability** planning.

### 4. Monitoring: Indicators & Methods

Monitoring uses three tiers of indicators mapped to the logic model:

- **Input indicators** — funds spent, staff trained, materials produced.
- **Process/output indicators** — sessions held, participants reached, coverage, fidelity to protocol.
- **Outcome indicators** — behaviour change (physical-activity levels), physiological change (BP, HbA1c, BMI), knowledge/attitude change.
- **Impact indicators** — morbidity, disability, quality of life (longer term).

Methods include attendance registers, checklists, supervisory visits, KAP (Knowledge-Attitude-Practice) surveys, dashboards (increasingly digital), and community feedback. A **monitoring framework** specifies each indicator's definition, data source, frequency and responsible person.

### 5. Setting 1 — Schools (Health Promoting Schools)

*Implementation:* embed physical activity and posture/back-care education in the curriculum, train teachers, engage parents, modify the environment (playgrounds, ergonomic furniture, safe school routes), and align with school health policy. Physiotherapists deliver posture-screening camps and active-classroom breaks. *Monitoring:* attendance, fitness testing, posture-screening coverage, teacher-delivered session fidelity, and behaviour indicators (screen time, active travel).

### 6. Setting 2 — Workplaces (Healthy Workplaces)

*Implementation:* ergonomic risk assessment, workstation redesign, micro-break and stretch programmes, sit-stand policies, on-site exercise, and management commitment (WHO Healthy Workplace Model: physical environment, psychosocial environment, personal health resources, enterprise community involvement). *Monitoring:* participation rates, musculoskeletal-symptom prevalence, absenteeism/presenteeism, sedentary time.

### 7. Setting 3 — Health-Care Facilities (Health Promoting Hospitals & Primary Care)

*Implementation:* integrate brief interventions, exercise counselling and referral into routine consultations; establish fall clinics and cardiac/pulmonary rehab; make the facility itself health-promoting (smoke-free, accessible). *Monitoring:* proportion of eligible patients counselled, referral completion, exercise-programme adherence.

### 8. Setting 4 — Community & Home

*Implementation:* community mobilisation, home-hazard modification, community exercise groups, peer educators and outreach camps; work through frontline workers. *Monitoring:* household coverage, home-modification completion, group-attendance retention, community-level behaviour surveys.

The following table contrasts implementation and monitoring across settings:

| Setting | Key gatekeeper | Signature intervention | Priority monitoring indicators | Main challenge |
|---------|---------------|------------------------|--------------------------------|----------------|
| School | Head-teacher / education dept. | Active classrooms, posture screening | Coverage, fitness, session fidelity | Curriculum overload, teacher time |
| Workplace | HR / management | Ergonomics, stretch breaks | Participation, MSD symptoms, absenteeism | Management ROI concerns, shift work |
| Health facility | Medical superintendent | Exercise counselling, fall/rehab clinics | % counselled, referral completion, adherence | Staff time, clinical priority competition |
| Community/home | Panchayat, ASHA, elders | Home modification, exercise groups | Coverage, retention, behaviour change | Reaching disadvantaged, sustainability |

## Qd. Discuss the design and development of IEC (Information, Education, Communication) resources for a health-promotion programme. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**IEC — Information, Education and Communication** — is a strategic, multi-channel approach that combines communication methods and messages to inform, motivate and empower individuals and communities to adopt and sustain healthy behaviours. **Information** provides facts and awareness; **Education** builds knowledge, skills and understanding; **Communication** is the two-way exchange that fosters attitude and behaviour change.

**IEC resources (or materials)** are the tangible tools — posters, pamphlets, flip-charts, flashcards, videos, audio spots, banners, hoardings, models and, increasingly, digital assets (apps, WhatsApp/social-media content, e-modules) — through which IEC messages are delivered. Good IEC materials are the visible face of a health-promotion programme; poorly designed materials (dense text, culturally inappropriate imagery, jargon) waste resources and can mislead.

The evolution is: **IEC → BCC (Behaviour Change Communication) → SBCC (Social and Behaviour Change Communication)**, reflecting a shift from information delivery toward theory-driven, participatory, multi-level communication.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **Communication model (Shannon-Weaver / SMCR — Source-Message-Channel-Receiver, Berlo):** effective IEC requires a credible **source**, a clear **message**, an appropriate **channel**, and a defined **receiver/audience**, minimising "noise."
- **Health Belief Model & Social Cognitive Theory** shape message content (cues to action, self-efficacy, modelling).
- **Diffusion of Innovations** informs channel choice and opinion-leader engagement.
- **Audience segmentation and social marketing (the 4 Ps + the "P" of positioning)** guide tailoring.
- **Health literacy principles (Nutbeam)** demand functional, interactive and critical literacy-appropriate materials.

### 3. Principles of Good IEC Material Design — the "ACCA/SMART" attributes

Effective IEC materials should be:

- **Accurate & evidence-based** — technically correct, consistent with programme messages.
- **Audience-appropriate** — matched to age, literacy, language, culture, gender.
- **Attractive & attention-grabbing** — good visuals, colour, layout.
- **Simple & clear** — one main idea per material, plain language, minimal text.
- **Action-oriented** — a clear "call to action" and self-efficacy cues.
- **Culturally sensitive** — locally relevant imagery, idioms, dress, context.
- **Pre-tested** — validated with the target audience before mass production.

### 4. Systematic Steps in Designing & Developing IEC Resources

1. **Situational & audience analysis** — define the health problem, target audience(s), their knowledge/attitudes/practices (KAP), literacy and preferred channels.
2. **Set communication objectives** — SMART, behaviourally specific (e.g., "increase to 70% the proportion of diabetics doing 30 min daily walking in 6 months").
3. **Audience segmentation** — primary (those whose behaviour must change), secondary (influencers), tertiary (policy).
4. **Develop the message** — the "one key message" rule; positive framing; benefits over fear where possible.
5. **Select channels & material mix** — interpersonal, print, mass, mid-media (folk/street theatre), digital.
6. **Draft/produce prototype materials** — with graphic designers and subject experts.
7. **Pre-test** — comprehension, attractiveness, acceptability, persuasion, self-involvement (with a sample of the target audience); revise.
8. **Finalise, produce and distribute.**
9. **Train the users** (frontline workers) to use the materials.
10. **Monitor use and feedback; revise** in the next cycle.

### 5. Message Development — the "Seven Cs" of Communication

Good messages satisfy the Seven Cs: **Command attention, Cater to the heart and head, Clear, Consistent, Convey a benefit, Create trust, and Call to action.** Messages should be pre-coded to the audience's language and reading level.

### 6. Pre-testing IEC Materials

Pre-testing is non-negotiable. It assesses five dimensions (the classic pre-test criteria):

- **Comprehension** — is the message understood?
- **Attractiveness** — does it draw attention?
- **Acceptability** — is anything offensive or taboo?
- **Persuasion** — does it motivate the intended action?
- **Self-involvement / identification** — do people see themselves in it?

Methods include focus-group discussions, individual interviews, and gain-score KAP tests. Materials are revised until they pass.

### 7. Channel Selection & Material Types

| Channel type | Example IEC materials | Reach | Best for | Physiotherapy example |
|--------------|----------------------|-------|----------|-----------------------|
| Interpersonal | Flip-charts, flashcards, counselling cards | Low reach, high impact | Skills, motivation | Home exercise demo cards |
| Print | Posters, pamphlets, leaflets, booklets | Medium | Reinforcement, reference | Back-care leaflet |
| Mid-media / folk | Street plays, puppet shows, wall paintings | Local, high engagement | Rural, low-literacy | Community fall-prevention skit |
| Mass media | TV/radio spots, hoardings, newspaper | Very high reach | Awareness, agenda-setting | Physical-activity radio jingle |
| Digital | Apps, WhatsApp videos, social media, SMS | High, scalable | Youth, urban, tailoring | WhatsApp exercise video series |

*Figure: A "message funnel" diagram would show the target behaviour at the base, feeding upward through message → channel → material → pre-test → distribution, illustrating that every design decision must trace back to the behavioural objective.*

### 8. Common Pitfalls

Overloaded text, technical jargon, culturally inappropriate imagery, too many messages per material, no call to action, skipping pre-testing, and one-way (non-participatory) design. Physiotherapy exercise materials frequently fail because exercise diagrams are unclear or not literacy-appropriate — described, step-numbered visuals with local models perform better.

## Qe. Describe the planning process for IEC interventions in a community health-promotion programme. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Planning** is the systematic, rational process of deciding *what* to do, *for whom*, *how*, *with what resources*, and *how success will be judged*, before implementation begins. **IEC planning** is the strategic communication-planning process that ensures information, education and communication activities are audience-centred, theory-driven, evidence-based, coordinated and evaluable, rather than ad-hoc.

Poorly planned IEC ("a poster here, a talk there") wastes resources and rarely changes behaviour. Systematic planning — often called **communication planning** or **strategic health communication planning** — aligns messages, channels, materials, timing and budget with defined behavioural objectives and the audience's realities. For the community physiotherapist, IEC planning turns a vague intent ("promote exercise") into a costed, monitorable communication strategy.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

Several planning models converge on similar logic:

- **PRECEDE-PROCEED (Green & Kreuter)** — the gold-standard planning model: PRECEDE phases (social, epidemiological, behavioural/environmental, educational/ecological, administrative/policy assessment) diagnose *what* to change and *why*; PROCEED phases plan implementation and evaluation. IEC planning maps directly onto its **predisposing, reinforcing and enabling factors**.
- **P-Process (Johns Hopkins CCP)** — the classic strategic communication-planning cycle: **Analyse → Design Strategy → Develop & Pretest → Implement & Monitor → Evaluate & Replan** (with capacity-strengthening and participation throughout).
- **Social marketing (4 Ps + audience insight)** — Product, Price, Place, Promotion.
- **ACADA / COMBI (Communication for Behavioural Impact, WHO)** — Assessment, Communication Analysis, Design, Action.
- **Logic model / results chain** for the M&E component.

### 3. Step 1 — Situational Analysis & Needs Assessment

Define the health problem using epidemiological data; conduct a **communication/audience analysis** (KAP survey, focus groups) to understand current knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, practices, barriers, media habits and trusted sources. Map stakeholders and existing IEC efforts (to avoid duplication). This corresponds to PRECEDE's social, epidemiological and educational diagnoses.

### 4. Step 2 — Audience Segmentation & Prioritisation

Divide the population into homogeneous segments (by risk, demographics, readiness/stage of change, media access). Identify **primary** (behaviour must change), **secondary** (influencers — family, opinion leaders) and **tertiary** (policy/gatekeeper) audiences. Prioritise segments by need and reachability.

### 5. Step 3 — Setting Communication Objectives

Translate the goal into **SMART, behaviourally specific communication objectives** distinguishing cognitive (awareness/knowledge), affective (attitude/intention) and behavioural (action/maintenance) objectives. Example: *"Within 6 months, increase from 30% to 60% the proportion of adults aged >40 in the catchment who correctly identify 30 minutes of daily walking as protective against diabetes, and increase self-reported walking to 50%."*

### 6. Step 4 — Strategy Design: Message, Channel & Material Mix

Decide the **positioning** and **key messages** (theory-driven — HBM cues, SCT self-efficacy), the **channel mix** (interpersonal + mid-media + mass + digital), the **material types**, the **spokespeople/opinion leaders**, and the **tone/creative concept**. Build an integrated, mutually reinforcing mix rather than isolated products.

### 7. Step 5 — Operational Planning: Budget, Roles, Timeline

Prepare a detailed **work plan / Gantt chart**, budget (production, distribution, airtime, training), human-resource allocation (who does what), procurement, and a distribution/dissemination plan. Plan **capacity building** of frontline deliverers. This is PRECEDE-PROCEED's administrative and policy assessment (resource, ability, policy, regulation, organisation).

### 8. Step 6 — Pre-testing & Finalisation

Pre-test draft messages and materials with the target audience (comprehension, attractiveness, acceptability, persuasion, self-involvement); revise; finalise and produce.

### 9. Step 7 — Implementation Plan & Monitoring/Evaluation Plan

Plan the roll-out (phasing, launch event, media schedule) and, critically, build the **M&E plan into the design from the start**: define process indicators (materials distributed, sessions held, reach), outcome indicators (KAP change), and impact indicators; specify data sources, methods, frequency and responsibility. Plan for **replanning** based on feedback (the cyclical nature of P-Process).

The following table maps a planning model to IEC actions:

| Planning phase (P-Process) | PRECEDE-PROCEED equivalent | Key IEC actions | Output |
|----------------------------|----------------------------|-----------------|--------|
| Analyse | Social/epi/behavioural/educational diagnosis | KAP survey, audience & stakeholder analysis | Situation & audience profile |
| Design strategy | Educational & ecological + admin diagnosis | Objectives, segments, message & channel mix, budget | Communication strategy document |
| Develop & pre-test | — | Draft materials, pre-test, revise | Finalised materials |
| Implement & monitor | Implementation (Process) | Roll-out, distribute, monitor reach & fidelity | Process/monitoring data |
| Evaluate & replan | Impact & outcome evaluation | Measure KAP/behaviour change, feed back | Evaluation report, revised plan |

*Figure: The P-Process is best drawn as a continuous loop (Analyse → Strategic Design → Develop/Pretest → Implement/Monitor → Evaluate/Replan) encircling a central hub labelled "Participation + Capacity Strengthening," emphasising that planning is iterative and community-owned, not linear.*

## Qf. Describe the implementation and monitoring of health education interventions. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Health education** is defined by the WHO as *"any combination of learning experiences designed to help individuals and communities improve their health, by increasing their knowledge or influencing their attitudes."* Green & Kreuter define it as *"any planned combination of learning experiences designed to predispose, enable and reinforce voluntary behaviour conducive to health."* It is a core component of health promotion, focused on informed, voluntary behaviour change through education.

**Implementation** of a health education intervention is the process of delivering the planned learning experiences to the target group with fidelity and appropriate adaptation. **Monitoring** is the ongoing tracking of activities, coverage and quality to ensure the intervention runs as planned and to enable timely correction. For the community physiotherapist, health education interventions include exercise-education classes, back-school programmes, diabetes/cardiac self-management education, fall-prevention teaching and antenatal/postnatal exercise education.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **PRECEDE-PROCEED** — health education targets **predisposing** (knowledge, attitudes, beliefs), **enabling** (skills, resources) and **reinforcing** (social support, feedback) factors; implementation is the PROCEED "implementation" phase.
- **Adult learning theory (Knowles' andragogy)** — adults learn best when learning is relevant, experiential, problem-centred and self-directed.
- **Bloom's taxonomy** — objectives span cognitive, affective and psychomotor domains (crucial for physiotherapy skills teaching).
- **Behaviour-change theories** — HBM, SCT, Transtheoretical (Stages of Change) — guide content and sequencing.
- **RE-AIM** — for implementation and monitoring/evaluation in real-world settings.
- **Kirkpatrick's four levels (reaction, learning, behaviour, results)** — for evaluating educational impact.

### 3. Pre-Implementation Requirements

Successful implementation depends on prior planning: defined **learning objectives** (SMART, across cognitive/affective/psychomotor domains), a **curriculum/lesson plan**, appropriate **teaching-learning methods** (lecture, demonstration, role-play, group discussion, return-demonstration), **IEC materials/teaching aids**, trained **educators**, a suitable **venue/schedule**, and a **monitoring plan**.

### 4. Implementation Process

1. **Mobilise and orient** the target group and secure participation.
2. **Deliver learning experiences** using varied, interactive, adult-learning methods matched to objectives and audience literacy.
3. **Ensure fidelity** to the curriculum while adapting to the group's needs and pace.
4. **Use appropriate teaching aids** (flip-charts, models, demonstrations, videos).
5. **Provide skills practice and return-demonstration** (essential for psychomotor/exercise learning) with corrective feedback.
6. **Reinforce** through repetition, take-home materials, follow-up and social support.
7. **Document** attendance, activities and immediate feedback.

Choice of method matters: WHO/education evidence shows retention rises from passive (lecture ~5–10% retention) to active methods (practice-by-doing and teach-others ~75–90%) — the "learning pyramid" concept — so demonstration and return-demonstration are prioritised in physiotherapy education.

### 5. Monitoring: What, How and When

Monitoring tracks the intervention against its plan across three domains:

- **Input monitoring** — educators trained, materials available, venue ready.
- **Process/activity monitoring** — number of sessions held vs planned, attendance/coverage, participant engagement, fidelity to curriculum, quality of facilitation (supervisory checklist).
- **Immediate output monitoring** — knowledge/skill acquisition at session end (quiz, return-demonstration checklist), participant satisfaction.

Methods: attendance registers, session checklists, supervisory/observation visits, pre-post knowledge tests, skills checklists, feedback forms and, increasingly, digital tracking. Monitoring should be **regular, participatory and used for action** (data for decision-making), not merely record-keeping.

### 6. Monitoring vs Evaluation (and Kirkpatrick levels)

Monitoring is continuous and process-focused; evaluation is periodic and outcome/impact-focused. Kirkpatrick's levels structure educational evaluation:

| Kirkpatrick level | Question answered | Indicator | Method |
|-------------------|-------------------|-----------|--------|
| 1. Reaction | Did they like it? | Satisfaction score | Feedback form |
| 2. Learning | Did they learn? | Knowledge/skill gain | Pre-post test, return-demo checklist |
| 3. Behaviour | Do they apply it? | Behaviour change | Follow-up survey, observation |
| 4. Results | Did health improve? | Clinical/health outcome | BP/HbA1c, morbidity, QoL |

*Figure: A monitoring dashboard could be sketched showing traffic-light indicators (green/amber/red) for coverage, session completion, fidelity and knowledge-gain, allowing a supervisor to spot at a glance which sub-centres need support.*

### 7. Using Monitoring Data for Course Correction

The value of monitoring lies in **feedback loops**: low attendance → reschedule or change venue; poor comprehension → simplify materials/method; low fidelity → retrain educators. Regular review meetings and supportive supervision convert data into action.
