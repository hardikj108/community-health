# Chapter 5 — Community-Based Approach to Healthcare

*MPT Community Physiotherapy · Long-Answer Model Notes*

The community-based approach to healthcare represents a decisive philosophical and operational shift away from a purely biomedical, hospital-centred, expert-driven model of care towards a model in which **communities are active partners** — and increasingly the primary agents — in the planning, delivery, and governance of their own health and rehabilitation. This shift was crystallised in the **Alma-Ata Declaration (1978)**, which proclaimed "Health for All", defined **Primary Health Care (PHC)** as the vehicle to achieve it, and made **community participation** a non-negotiable pillar; it was renewed and modernised forty years later by the **Astana Declaration (2018)** and the drive towards Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). For people with disabilities, the same logic produced **Community-Based Rehabilitation (CBR)** — launched by WHO after Alma-Ata — and its evolution into **Community-Based Inclusive Development (CBID)**, structured today around the five-domain **WHO CBR Matrix** (health, education, livelihood, social, and empowerment) and anchored in the human-rights framework of the **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD, 2006)**.

For the postgraduate community physiotherapist, mastery of this domain means being able to move fluently between concept and practice: to justify participation using **Arnstein's ladder of citizen participation**, to engage and mobilise communities using evidence-based methods, to implement health promotion through participatory platforms, and to plan, manage, and evaluate CBR/CBID programmes within India's decentralised **Panchayati Raj**, **National Health Mission (NHM)**, ASHA/VHSNC, and **Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act 2016** ecosystem. This chapter addresses seven 20-mark long-answer questions:

- **Qa.** Community Participation as a Fundamental Component of Primary Health Care
- **Qb.** Strategies for Community Engagement in Healthcare Delivery
- **Qc.** Implementing Health Promotion Through Community Participation
- **Qd.** Evidence-Based Strategies for Community Mobilization and Participation
- **Qe.** Community-Based Rehabilitation (CBR) as a Strategy Within Community Development for People with Disabilities
- **Qf.** Understanding and Implementing WHO CBR Guidelines
- **Qg.** Planning and Management of CBR Programs

## Qa. Discuss community participation as a fundamental component of Primary Health Care. Explain its theoretical basis, levels, determinants, benefits and challenges, with reference to the Alma-Ata and Astana Declarations. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Primary Health Care (PHC)** was defined by the **Alma-Ata Declaration (1978)** as "essential health care based on practical, scientifically sound and socially acceptable methods and technology made universally accessible to individuals and families in the community through their full participation and at a cost that the community and country can afford to maintain at every stage of their development in the spirit of self-reliance and self-determination." Embedded within this definition are two structural pillars that separate PHC from ordinary medical care: **universal accessibility** and **community participation**. Community participation is therefore not an optional add-on to PHC — it is constitutive of it.

**Community participation** (used interchangeably with community involvement, community engagement, and, at its highest expression, community empowerment) is defined by WHO as *"a process by which individuals and families assume responsibility for their own health and welfare and for those of the community, and develop the capacity to contribute to their and the community's development."* It implies that people, acting individually or collectively, take part in the **assessment, planning, implementation, management, monitoring and evaluation** of health activities that affect them, and increasingly share in the **control of resources and decisions**.

A **community** is a group of people sharing a common location, identity, culture, interest, or condition (e.g., a village, an urban slum, or a disability self-help group), possessing shared norms and a degree of collective agency. Distinguishing the concept from tokenism is essential: genuine participation involves a redistribution of power, whereas "pseudo-participation" merely uses the community as free labour or as a rubber stamp for pre-decided plans.

The **Astana Declaration (2018)**, adopted at the Global Conference on Primary Health Care marking Alma-Ata's 40th anniversary, reaffirmed community participation as central and sharpened it for the era of **Universal Health Coverage (UHC)** and the **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**. It committed signatories to "empower individuals and communities... and to involve them in the development and implementation of policies and plans that have an impact on health," explicitly recognising communities as co-producers rather than passive recipients of care.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

The intellectual scaffolding for community participation draws on several complementary frameworks:

- **Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation (1969):** The foundational typology, describing eight rungs grouped into three bands — *non-participation* (manipulation, therapy), *tokenism* (informing, consultation, placation), and *citizen power* (partnership, delegated power, citizen control). It provides the diagnostic lens for judging whether a "participatory" health programme is genuine or cosmetic, and reminds practitioners that the goal is to climb from tokenism towards partnership and control.
- **Rifkin's Spider-Gram / Pentagram of Participation:** Purpose-built for health, it assesses participation across five process dimensions — **needs assessment, leadership, organisation, resource mobilisation, and management** — plotting each on a continuum from narrow/professional-led to wide/community-led, producing a visual "web" of the breadth of participation.
- **The Continuum of Participation:** A simpler practitioner model running from *information → consultation → collaboration → co-production → community control*, echoing the IAP2 (International Association for Public Participation) spectrum (Inform, Consult, Involve, Collaborate, Empower).
- **Empowerment theory (Freire, Wallerstein, Laverack):** Frames participation as a route to **critical consciousness (conscientisation)** and the building of **community capacity** and **collective efficacy**, so that health improvement flows from communities acting on the social determinants of their own health.
- **WHO's PHC pillars:** The renewed 2018/2020 PHC operational framework rests on three components — *integrated health services*, *multisectoral policy and action*, and *empowered people and communities* — the last being community participation institutionalised.

*Figure: Arnstein's ladder can be visualised as an eight-runged staircase. The bottom two rungs (manipulation, therapy) sit in a shaded "non-participation" zone; the middle three (informing, consultation, placation) in a "tokenism" zone; and the top three (partnership, delegated power, citizen control) in a "citizen power" zone. Health programmes should be plotted against this staircase to expose where power actually lies.*

### 3. Rationale — Why Participation Is Fundamental to PHC

Community participation is fundamental to PHC for interlocking philosophical, epidemiological, and pragmatic reasons:

- **Health is socially produced.** Most determinants of health — water, sanitation, nutrition, housing, income, gender norms, behaviour — lie outside the clinic and inside the community. PHC can only address them with the community's engagement.
- **Sustainability and ownership.** Programmes designed *with* communities are more likely to be used, maintained, and defended after external funding ends. Ownership converts beneficiaries into stakeholders.
- **Relevance and cultural fit.** Communities know their own priorities, taboos, and trusted channels; participation aligns services with felt needs rather than professionally assumed needs.
- **Equity.** Participation gives voice to the marginalised (women, the poor, people with disabilities), countering the inverse-care law whereby those most in need get least.
- **Accountability.** Community oversight of resources and personnel reduces leakage and absenteeism and improves responsiveness — a core function of bodies like India's Village Health Sanitation and Nutrition Committees.
- **Resource mobilisation.** Communities contribute labour, local knowledge, land, money, and social capital, extending scarce public resources.

### 4. Levels / Typology of Community Participation

Participation operates at graded intensities. The table below synthesises the dominant typologies and maps them onto physiotherapy-relevant examples.

| Level (ascending) | Nature of power | Community role | Community-physiotherapy example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manipulation / Passivity | None (non-participation) | Recipients told what will happen | Villagers merely informed a rehab camp will occur |
| Information | Minimal | Receive one-way messages | Health talks on stroke prevention, no dialogue |
| Consultation | Low | Views sought, no guarantee of use | Survey of felt disability needs before planning |
| Collaboration / Partnership | Shared | Joint planning and delivery | VHSNC co-designs a fall-prevention programme with the PT |
| Co-production / Delegated power | High | Community leads with support | Self-help group runs a CBR day-centre, PT as advisor |
| Community control / Empowerment | Full | Community owns decisions & resources | Disabled People's Organisation governs and funds services |

A useful complementary lens is the **"5 M" typology of what people participate in**: participation in **needs assessment, planning, mobilising resources, management/implementation, and monitoring/evaluation**. Genuine PHC seeks participation across *all five*, not merely in implementation (the commonest — and weakest — form, where communities supply labour but no decision-making).

### 5. Determinants / Enabling Factors

Whether participation flourishes depends on:

- **Political will and decentralisation** — legal devolution of authority and funds to local bodies (e.g., Panchayati Raj), a supportive policy environment.
- **Trust and social capital** — pre-existing networks, cohesion, and confidence between community and health system.
- **Capacity** — literacy, health literacy, leadership, and the skills to plan and manage.
- **Genuine sharing of power and information** — transparency of budgets, data, and decisions.
- **Inclusive representation** — deliberate inclusion of women, the poor, and persons with disabilities; avoidance of elite capture.
- **Responsive, respectful providers** — health workers who see communities as partners, supported by continuous facilitation.
- **Time and continuity** — participation is a slow, relational process, undermined by short project cycles.

### 6. Benefits of Community Participation

- Improved **coverage, uptake, and equity** of services.
- Greater **relevance and acceptability** of interventions.
- Enhanced **sustainability and local ownership**.
- Strengthened **accountability and governance**.
- **Capacity building and empowerment**, with spill-over into other development.
- Better **health outcomes** — evidence links participatory approaches to reduced maternal and neonatal mortality (e.g., participatory women's groups), improved immunisation, and better chronic-disease self-management.
- **Cost-effectiveness** through resource sharing and demand-driven design.

### 7. Challenges, Risks & Critiques

- **Tokenism** — programmes labelled participatory that remain on Arnstein's lower rungs.
- **Elite capture** — dominant castes, genders, or families monopolising committees, excluding the marginalised.
- **Participation fatigue and opportunity cost** — the poor cannot afford unpaid time.
- **Unequal power** between professionals and lay members, reproducing dependency.
- **Co-optation and instrumentalism** — using communities to cut costs rather than to empower.
- **Sustainability without support** — committees that atrophy once external facilitation withdraws.
- **Measurement difficulty** — participation is hard to quantify and evaluate.
- **Medical dominance and provider resistance** — clinicians reluctant to cede authority.

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

India institutionalised PHC-era participation well before Alma-Ata (the Bhore Committee, 1946) and has since built one of the world's largest participatory health architectures under the **National Health Mission (NHM, 2005)**. Key mechanisms:

- **ASHA (Accredited Social Health Activist):** ~one million community-selected, community-accountable women health volunteers — the human face of participatory PHC, linking villages to sub-centres and Health & Wellness Centres.
- **Village Health, Sanitation and Nutrition Committee (VHSNC):** A statutory sub-committee of the Gram Panchayat that plans local health action and manages an **untied fund (~₹10,000/year)**, enabling communities to prioritise and spend on local health needs — a concrete step towards Arnstein's "delegated power".
- **Rogi Kalyan Samiti / Jan Arogya Samiti (Patient Welfare Committee):** Community-cum-facility bodies governing PHCs/CHCs and the new Ayushman Arogya Mandirs, receiving untied grants to improve facilities.
- **Panchayati Raj Institutions (73rd & 74th Constitutional Amendments, 1992/93):** Three-tier decentralised local self-government (Gram Panchayat, Panchayat Samiti, Zila Parishad) with health as a devolved subject, the legal basis for community control.
- **Community Action for Health (CAH)** and **Jan Samwad/Jan Sunwai (public hearings), community score-cards, and social audits** operationalise community-based monitoring and accountability, championed by the National/State Health Systems Resource Centres and civil-society partners.
- Landmark Indian evidence: participatory learning-and-action **women's group** trials in Jharkhand and Odisha (Ekjut) demonstrated large reductions in neonatal mortality — cited in the Lancet as proof of participation's clinical impact.

India's shift from ~1.5 lakh Sub-Centres/PHCs to **Ayushman Bharat Health & Wellness Centres (Ayushman Arogya Mandirs)** explicitly revives the "comprehensive PHC + community engagement" vision of Alma-Ata for the UHC era.

## Qb. Describe the strategies for community engagement in healthcare delivery. Discuss the spectrum, methods, stakeholders and steps involved, with special reference to community physiotherapy. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Community engagement (CE)** is defined by WHO as *"a process of developing relationships that enable stakeholders to work together to address health-related issues and promote well-being to achieve positive health impact and outcomes."* The US CDC/ATSDR classic definition frames it as *"the process of working collaboratively with and through groups of people affiliated by geographic proximity, special interest, or similar situations to address issues affecting the well-being of those people."* Whereas *community participation* (Qa) emphasises the community's role in **its own** health, community engagement emphasises the **strategies and relational processes** the health system uses to build that participation. In practice the two are two sides of the same coin: engagement is the *how*, participation the *state achieved*.

**Healthcare delivery** encompasses the full continuum — promotion, prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliation — delivered across community, primary, secondary, and tertiary levels. Engaging communities in *delivery* means involving them not merely as recipients but as co-planners, co-providers (e.g., peer educators, community volunteers, family caregivers in rehabilitation), and co-evaluators.

For the **community physiotherapist**, engagement strategies are the practical toolkit for identifying persons with movement impairment and disability who never reach the clinic, delivering rehabilitation at home and in community venues, training caregivers, forming self-help groups, and building demand for and accountability of rehabilitation services.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

Community engagement strategy selection is guided by the intensity of collaboration sought. The dominant framework is the **IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation**, running from **Inform → Consult → Involve → Collaborate → Empower**, each with a defined promise to the public. This maps directly onto Arnstein's ladder (Qa) and onto the **CDC principles of community engagement** (know the community; establish relationships and trust; work through existing structures and leaders; ensure partnership and shared decision-making; mobilise community assets; release control to the community; commit for the long term).

Complementary frameworks include:

- **Social ecological / socio-ecological model** — engagement must target multiple levels (individual, interpersonal, organisational, community, policy) simultaneously.
- **Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD)** — start from a community's existing strengths, skills, and networks rather than its deficits.
- **Diffusion of Innovations (Rogers)** — use opinion leaders and early adopters to spread health innovations through social networks.
- **Community Coalition Action Theory** — explains how multi-stakeholder coalitions form, function, and produce health change.
- **Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR)** — an equitable partnership model for co-producing knowledge and interventions with communities.

### 3. Principles Underpinning Effective Engagement

Before listing strategies, the physiotherapist must internalise the enabling principles: **trust and respect; cultural humility and appropriateness; two-way communication; inclusiveness and equity (deliberately reaching women, the poor, and persons with disabilities); transparency; working through existing community structures; asset-based thinking; flexibility; and long-term commitment.** Engagement that violates these — that is extractive, one-off, or tokenistic — erodes trust and is often worse than none.

### 4. The Spectrum of Engagement Strategies

The table maps the IAP2 spectrum to concrete strategies and community-physiotherapy applications.

| IAP2 level | Goal / promise | Representative strategies | Community-physiotherapy application |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inform** | Provide balanced information | Health education talks, posters/IEC, mass & social media, street plays (nukkad natak) | Awareness on stroke warning signs, fall prevention, cerebral-palsy early identification |
| **Consult** | Obtain feedback | Community surveys, focus-group discussions, key-informant interviews, suggestion boxes, transect walks | Participatory disability needs assessment; mapping barriers to access |
| **Involve** | Work directly with community | Community volunteers, caregiver training, peer support, participatory workshops | Training family caregivers in home exercise; volunteer disability screeners |
| **Collaborate** | Partner in decisions | Coalitions, advisory boards, VHSNC/RKS partnership, co-design/co-production | Co-planning a community fall-prevention or CBR programme with the panchayat |
| **Empower** | Community decides | Delegated budgets, community-managed programmes, DPO/SHG control | A Disabled People's Organisation governing a community rehabilitation centre |

### 5. Key Strategies for Community Engagement in Delivery

#### 5.1 Community mapping, entry, and rapport building
Gaining legitimate **entry** through recognised gatekeepers (panchayat, religious/community leaders, ASHA/anganwadi workers), conducting **social and resource mapping**, transect walks, and participatory appraisal to understand the community's structure, assets, needs, and power dynamics *before* intervening.

#### 5.2 Engaging and training community health workers, volunteers, and peers
Selecting, training, and supporting **local intermediaries** — ASHAs, community-based rehabilitation workers (CBR workers), peer educators, and volunteers — who share the community's language and culture, extend reach, and build trust. Task-shifting appropriate rehabilitation tasks (e.g., supervised home exercise, mobility training) to trained CBR workers under physiotherapist supervision is a core delivery strategy.

#### 5.3 Forming and strengthening community groups and committees
Catalysing **self-help groups (SHGs)**, mothers'/women's groups, **Disabled People's Organisations (DPOs)**, and working through statutory bodies (VHSNC, Jan Arogya Samiti) to plan, deliver, and sustain services — the participatory-learning-and-action (PLA) cycle of *identify → prioritise → plan → act → evaluate* is a proven engagement engine.

#### 5.4 Participatory needs assessment and priority setting
Using **PRA/PLA tools** (mapping, ranking, seasonal calendars, problem trees, Venn diagrams) so the community defines its own health and rehabilitation priorities, generating ownership from the outset.

#### 5.5 Communication, IEC/BCC, and social mobilisation
Deploying **Information–Education–Communication (IEC)** and **Behaviour-Change Communication (BCC)** through culturally resonant channels — folk media, street theatre, community radio, mid-media, and increasingly digital/social media and mHealth — to inform, motivate, and mobilise.

#### 5.6 Multisectoral coalition building and partnerships
Building **coalitions** across health, education, social welfare, panchayati raj, NGOs, and the private sector — essential for the multisectoral action PHC and CBR require (e.g., linking rehabilitation with disability certification, assistive devices, education, and livelihood).

#### 5.7 Community-based monitoring, accountability, and feedback
Instituting **community score-cards, social audits, public hearings (jan sunwai), and citizen report cards** so communities monitor service quality and hold providers accountable — climbing towards the empowerment end of the spectrum.

#### 5.8 Empowerment and capacity building
Ultimately transferring skills, knowledge, and control — leadership training, financial literacy, and delegated management — so communities and DPOs sustain services independently.

### 6. Stakeholders in Community Engagement

Effective engagement is a stakeholder ecosystem: **community members and families; persons with disabilities and their DPOs; community leaders and gatekeepers (panchayat, religious, traditional); community health workers (ASHA, anganwadi, CBR workers); local self-government (Panchayati Raj); the formal health system (HWCs, PHCs, physiotherapists, medical officers); NGOs and civil society; the education, social-welfare and livelihood sectors; and funders/government.** Stakeholder analysis (mapping interest vs. influence) helps target engagement effort.

### 7. Steps in a Community Engagement Process

1. **Prepare** — situational/stakeholder analysis, define purpose and level of engagement, secure resources and mandate.
2. **Enter and build trust** — approach through gatekeepers, transparency about intent and limits.
3. **Assess needs participatorily** — PRA/PLA, community mapping.
4. **Plan jointly** — co-design objectives, roles, and resource contributions.
5. **Implement** — deliver through community structures and workers.
6. **Monitor and give feedback** — community-based monitoring, score-cards.
7. **Evaluate and sustain** — review, celebrate, institutionalise, and progressively hand over control.

### 8. Barriers and Facilitators

*Barriers:* mistrust and past extractive experiences; language and cultural gaps; elite capture and exclusion; provider dominance and reluctance; time and resource constraints; short project cycles; poor coordination; low health literacy; stigma (especially around disability). *Facilitators:* trusted local intermediaries; genuine power sharing; cultural competence; continuity and adequate resourcing; supportive policy/decentralisation; use of existing structures and assets; and monitoring for equity.

## Qc. Explain how health promotion is implemented through community participation. Discuss the Ottawa Charter, planning models, and the physiotherapist's role, with examples. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Health promotion** is defined by WHO's **Ottawa Charter (1986)** as *"the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health."* It moves beyond behaviour-focused health education to address the **social, economic, and environmental determinants** of health, and it positions **community action** as one of its central strategies. Health promotion is thus intrinsically participatory: people cannot increase *control* over their health unless they participate in the decisions and actions that shape it.

**Community participation in health promotion** means that communities are engaged across the whole cycle — identifying their own health priorities, planning and implementing promotional actions, mobilising resources, and evaluating results — so that health-promoting environments and behaviours are created *by* communities rather than *for* them. This aligns health promotion with the Alma-Ata/Astana PHC vision (Qa) and with the empowerment end of the engagement spectrum (Qb).

For the **community physiotherapist**, health promotion through participation is the primary vehicle for population-level prevention of the movement-related burden of disease — physical inactivity, falls, low back pain, osteoarthritis, stroke, and disability — through community-designed activity programmes, ergonomic and fall-safe environments, and disability-inclusive promotion.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **Ottawa Charter (1986) — five action areas:** (1) *Build healthy public policy*; (2) *Create supportive environments*; (3) **Strengthen community action**; (4) *Develop personal skills*; (5) *Reorient health services*. "Strengthen community action" explicitly names participation — *"health promotion works through concrete and effective community action in setting priorities, making decisions, planning strategies and implementing them to achieve better health. At the heart of this process is the empowerment of communities."* Three basic strategies underpin these: **enable, mediate, advocate.**
- **Successor declarations:** *Adelaide (1988, healthy public policy)*, *Sundsvall (1991, supportive environments)*, *Jakarta (1997, priorities incl. partnerships)*, *Bangkok Charter (2005, globalised health promotion)*, and *Shanghai (2016)* — the last strongly reaffirming health promotion, health literacy, and healthy cities within the SDGs.
- **PRECEDE-PROCEED model (Green & Kreuter):** The pre-eminent participatory planning framework — PRECEDE (Predisposing, Reinforcing, Enabling Constructs in Educational/Environmental Diagnosis and Evaluation) diagnoses needs through social, epidemiological, behavioural/environmental, educational/ecological, and administrative/policy assessment; PROCEED (Policy, Regulatory, Organizational Constructs in Educational and Environmental Development) guides implementation and process/impact/outcome evaluation. Its first phase is a *social assessment* driven by community participation.
- **Settings approach:** Promotes health where people live, work, learn, and play — *Healthy Cities/Villages, Health-Promoting Schools and Workplaces* — inherently participatory.
- **RE-AIM** (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) for evaluating the population impact and sustainability of community health-promotion programmes.
- **Health Impact Assessment (HIA)** and **social capital / empowerment theory** as supporting constructs.

*Figure: The Ottawa Charter is classically drawn as a circle ("health promotion logo") — a dot (the individual) within a circle divided into the five action areas, encircled by the three strategies enable–mediate–advocate, all resting on a rainbow arch. Community action sits at the interface between individual skills and healthy public policy.*

### 3. Why Community Participation Is Essential to Health Promotion

- Health promotion targets **determinants** that only communities can change (environments, norms, policies).
- **Sustainability**: promoted behaviours and environments persist only with community ownership.
- **Equity**: participation reaches and includes the marginalised, reducing health inequities.
- **Empowerment** is both a means and an *end* of health promotion — building "community competence" and control.
- **Cultural relevance**: community-designed promotion fits local values and channels.

### 4. Implementing Health Promotion Through Participation — The Process

The table maps the Ottawa action areas to participatory implementation and physiotherapy examples.

| Ottawa action area | Participatory implementation | Community-physiotherapy example |
|---|---|---|
| Build healthy public policy | Community advocacy for policy (gram sabha resolutions, accessibility bylaws) | Advocacy for barrier-free public buildings and footpaths |
| Create supportive environments | Community-built safe, active, accessible spaces | Community exercise parks, safe walking paths, fall-safe homes |
| Strengthen community action | SHGs/committees set priorities, plan, act, evaluate (PLA cycle) | Village group runs a fall-prevention & active-ageing programme |
| Develop personal skills | Participatory education, peer-led skill training | Caregiver & self-management training for back pain/arthritis |
| Reorient health services | Communities shape prevention-oriented, accessible services | HWC physiotherapy reoriented to community outreach & screening |

A practical **participatory implementation cycle** integrates PRECEDE-PROCEED with the PLA loop:

1. **Participatory social & needs assessment** — community defines quality-of-life concerns and health priorities (PRECEDE phase 1–2; PRA/PLA tools).
2. **Behavioural, environmental & educational diagnosis** — jointly identify modifiable determinants and predisposing/enabling/reinforcing factors.
3. **Co-planning** — set objectives, choose strategies across the five action areas, allocate roles and resources (administrative/policy assessment).
4. **Capacity building & mobilisation** — train peer educators/volunteers; mobilise local resources and coalitions.
5. **Implementation** — deliver through community structures and settings.
6. **Participatory monitoring & evaluation** — process, impact, outcome (PROCEED; RE-AIM); community reviews and adapts.
7. **Institutionalisation & scale-up** — embed in local governance for sustainability.

### 5. Methods and Approaches

- **Health education and BCC** delivered by and with the community (peer educators, folk media, street theatre).
- **Social mobilisation campaigns** — immunisation drives, active-living/Fit India events, disability inclusion melas.
- **Settings-based programmes** — Healthy Villages/Cities, Health-Promoting Schools, workplace ergonomics.
- **Community development and empowerment** — SHGs, women's PLA groups, DPOs building collective efficacy.
- **Advocacy** — community-led lobbying for healthy public policy and accessible environments.
- **Intersectoral action** — linking health with education, water/sanitation, agriculture, and urban planning.

### 6. Role of the Community Physiotherapist in Participatory Health Promotion

- **Educator and skill-builder** — teaches exercise, posture, ergonomics, fall prevention, and self-management, and trains peer/lay educators (developing personal skills).
- **Facilitator and mobiliser** — catalyses and supports community groups to plan and run activity/rehabilitation programmes (strengthen community action).
- **Environmental advisor** — advises on accessible, active, fall-safe built environments (create supportive environments).
- **Advocate** — champions disability inclusion, accessibility, and physical-activity policy (build healthy public policy; advocate).
- **Service re-orienter** — shifts physiotherapy from clinic-bound treatment to community-based prevention and screening (reorient health services).
- **Evaluator** — co-designs participatory monitoring using RE-AIM/PRECEDE-PROCEED metrics.

### 7. Challenges

Tokenistic participation; dominance of biomedical/curative priorities over promotion; short funding cycles; measurement difficulty; competing community priorities and participation fatigue; provider skill gaps in facilitation; and the risk of promoting individual behaviour change while ignoring structural determinants ("victim-blaming").

## Qd. Describe the evidence-based strategies for community mobilization and participation. Discuss the process, models, evidence of effectiveness and evaluation. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Community mobilisation** is defined by UNICEF/WHO as *"a capacity-building process through which community individuals, groups or organisations plan, carry out and evaluate activities on a participatory and sustained basis to improve their health and other conditions, either on their own initiative or stimulated by others."* It is the *active, organised, collective* end of the participation spectrum — moving a community from awareness to organised action. Whereas *engagement* (Qb) is the relational strategy and *participation* (Qa) the state achieved, **mobilisation is the mechanism** that converts participation into sustained collective action, marshalling human, material, and social resources towards a shared health goal.

**Evidence-based** here means strategies whose effectiveness is supported by systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and high-quality trials — not merely by convention. This is a crucial exam distinction: the postgraduate must cite *what works*, notably the strong evidence for **participatory learning-and-action (PLA) women's groups**, **community health workers**, and **community-based monitoring**.

For the **community physiotherapist**, mobilisation is how a scattered, often hidden population of persons with disability and chronic movement disorders is organised — through self-help groups, DPOs, and caregiver networks — into a force that identifies its members, demands services, and delivers peer support and community-based rehabilitation.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **Participatory Learning and Action (PLA) / Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA):** The methodological engine of mobilisation — a facilitated **community action cycle**: *(1) identify and prioritise problems → (2) plan strategies → (3) implement → (4) evaluate and share*, repeated in successive meetings. This is the framework behind the strongest global evidence (Prost et al., Lancet 2013).
- **Community Organisation Theory / Rothman's three models:** *Locality development* (broad participation, consensus, self-help), *social planning* (technical, expert-driven problem-solving), and *social action* (organising the disadvantaged to shift power). Mobilisation typically blends locality development and social action.
- **Diffusion of Innovations (Rogers)** and **social network theory** — mobilisation spreads through opinion leaders and existing ties.
- **Empowerment and social-capital theory (Freire, Putnam, Laverack)** — mobilisation builds collective efficacy, critical consciousness, and bonding/bridging social capital.
- **Community Coalition Action Theory** and **Community Readiness Model** — explain coalition function and stage-match mobilisation strategies to a community's readiness (from "no awareness" to "high ownership").
- **Social Ecological Model / RE-AIM** — for multi-level targeting and evaluation.

### 3. The Community Mobilisation Process (Action Cycle)

1. **Preparation & entry** — situational analysis, stakeholder mapping, building relationships with gatekeepers; assess **community readiness**.
2. **Awareness raising** — sensitise the community to the issue (e.g., preventable disability, falls).
3. **Organising the community** — form or strengthen groups/committees; identify and train local mobilisers and leaders.
4. **Participatory problem identification & prioritisation** — PLA tools to let the community name and rank its priorities.
5. **Participatory planning** — co-develop objectives, strategies, roles, timeline, and resource plan.
6. **Resource mobilisation** — marshal local (labour, money, materials, social capital) and external resources.
7. **Collective action / implementation** — the community executes the plan through its structures.
8. **Monitoring & participatory evaluation** — community tracks progress and reflects.
9. **Sustaining & scaling** — institutionalise in local governance; build federations/networks.

### 4. Evidence-Based Strategies for Mobilisation and Participation

| Strategy | What it involves | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **PLA participatory women's/community groups** | Facilitated action-cycle groups meeting regularly | **Strong** — meta-analysis shows large reductions in neonatal (and maternal) mortality when population coverage of groups is adequate |
| **Community health workers (CHWs)** | Trained local workers delivering promotion, care, and linkage | **Strong** — Cochrane/WHO evidence for improved immunisation, breastfeeding, TB, child health, and access |
| **Community-based monitoring & accountability** | Score-cards, social audits, public hearings | **Moderate–strong** — improves service quality, utilisation, and responsiveness |
| **Peer support / self-help groups** | Peer-led education and support, DPOs, SHGs | **Moderate** — improves self-management, empowerment, and, for disability, inclusion |
| **Community coalitions/partnerships** | Multi-stakeholder alliances for a shared goal | **Moderate** — effective when well-structured and resourced |
| **Mass/social mobilisation campaigns** | Drives, melas, folk & social media | **Moderate** — improves coverage of specific interventions (e.g., immunisation) |
| **Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR)** | Communities co-produce knowledge and interventions | **Moderate** — improves relevance, uptake, and equity |

**Key evidence anchors to cite:** Prost et al. (Lancet 2013) — PLA women's groups; Lewin et al. (Cochrane 2010) — lay/community health workers; O'Mara-Eves et al. (2015) — community engagement meta-analysis showing positive effects on health behaviours, outcomes, self-efficacy, and social support, especially for disadvantaged groups; Haldane et al. (2019) — systematic review linking community participation to empowerment and health outcomes.

### 5. Principles of Effective Mobilisation

Start from **community-defined priorities and assets**; work through **existing structures and trusted leaders**; ensure **inclusive, equitable representation** (guard against elite capture); build **capacity and leadership**; ensure **genuine power and resource sharing**; provide **continuous facilitation and support**; commit **long-term**; and **monitor for equity and unintended harms**. Mobilisation that is externally imposed, time-bound, or extractive predictably fails.

### 6. Evaluating Mobilisation and Participation

Evaluation must capture both *process* (breadth and depth of participation) and *outcomes*:

- **Process/participation measures:** Rifkin's spider-gram (five dimensions), Laverack's nine "domains of community empowerment" (participation, leadership, organisational structures, problem assessment, resource mobilisation, "asking why", links with others, role of outside agents, programme management), and community-readiness staging.
- **Outcome measures:** service coverage/utilisation, equity, health/disability outcomes, and empowerment/social-capital indices.
- **Frameworks:** **RE-AIM** (reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance) and realist evaluation (what works, for whom, in what context).
- **Methods:** mixed-methods — quantitative coverage/outcome data plus qualitative participatory evaluation (most-significant-change, community score-cards).

### 7. Barriers, Facilitators and Common Failures

*Facilitators:* trusted CHWs/mobilisers, decentralisation and political will, adequate coverage/"dose", cultural fit, continuity, and equity focus. *Barriers/failures:* insufficient population coverage (the commonest reason PLA groups fail to show effect), elite capture, tokenism, short project cycles, weak facilitation, poor integration with the health system, and neglect of the most marginalised — precisely the groups (persons with disability, women, the poor) mobilisation should prioritise.

## Qe. Discuss Community-Based Rehabilitation (CBR) as a strategy within community development for people with disabilities. Trace its evolution, principles, and shift to Community-Based Inclusive Development (CBID). (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Community-Based Rehabilitation (CBR)** was launched by WHO in 1978, in the wake of Alma-Ata, as a strategy to extend rehabilitation to the ~80% of the world's people with disabilities who lived in low-resource, mostly rural areas without access to institution-based services. The 2004 **Joint Position Paper (ILO, UNESCO, WHO)** provides the standard definition: CBR is *"a strategy within general community development for the rehabilitation, equalisation of opportunities, poverty reduction and social inclusion of all people with disabilities. CBR is implemented through the combined efforts of people with disabilities themselves, their families, organisations and communities, and the relevant governmental and non-governmental health, education, vocational, social and other services."*

Three phrases in this definition are load-bearing and must anchor the answer:

- **"a strategy within general community development"** — CBR is *not* a purely medical or therapy programme; it is a component of the broader development of the community, addressing poverty, education, and livelihood alongside health.
- **"equalisation of opportunities... social inclusion"** — its goal is participation and rights, not merely impairment reduction.
- **"combined efforts of people with disabilities themselves"** — it is participatory and empowering, driven by persons with disabilities and their organisations (DPOs).

**Disability** is understood through the WHO **International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF, 2001)** as the outcome of interaction between a health condition and **contextual (environmental and personal) factors** — a biopsychosocial, not purely medical, model. CBR operationalises this and the **social/human-rights model** enshrined in the **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD, 2006)**.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **Models of disability:** CBR embodies the shift from the **medical model** (disability as an individual deficit to be cured) to the **social model** (disability as created by societal barriers) and the **biopsychosocial model (ICF)**, culminating in the **human-rights model (UNCRPD)**.
- **CBR Matrix (WHO, 2010):** The organising framework — five **components** (Health, Education, Livelihood, Social, Empowerment), each with five **elements**, giving 25 cells. Crucially, only *one* of the five components is Health, underscoring CBR's developmental (not merely clinical) nature. Empowerment underpins the other four.
- **Community development theory** — CBR sits within locality development and social-action approaches (Rothman), building community capacity and inclusion.
- **Equalisation of opportunities** (UN Standard Rules on the Equalization of Opportunities for Persons with Disabilities, 1993) — the goal of making society's systems accessible to all.
- **Twin-track approach** — combining *disability-specific* interventions with *mainstreaming* disability into all development — the conceptual bridge from CBR to **CBID**.

*Figure: The CBR Matrix is drawn as a 5×5 grid. Columns = the five components (Health, Education, Livelihood, Social, Empowerment). Rows within each column = its five elements (e.g., under Health: promotion, prevention, medical care, rehabilitation, assistive devices). Empowerment (communication, community mobilisation, political participation, self-help groups, DPOs) is often shown as the base that enables the other four.*

### 3. Evolution of CBR

| Phase | Period | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin** | 1978 onward | WHO launches CBR post-Alma-Ata; Helander's *Training in the Community for People with Disabilities* manuals — technology-transfer, service-extension model delivered by families/local workers |
| **Expansion & critique** | 1980s–1990s | Rapid spread; criticised as top-down, medically dominated, and neglecting rights and DPO leadership |
| **Reconceptualisation** | 2004 | ILO/UNESCO/WHO Joint Position Paper reframes CBR as **community development, rights, poverty reduction, and inclusion**, with PWD/DPO leadership |
| **Consolidation** | 2010 | **WHO CBR Guidelines & CBR Matrix** published — comprehensive, multisectoral, rights-based; aligned with UNCRPD (2006) |
| **CBID** | 2015 onward | Evolution to **Community-Based Inclusive Development** — disability-inclusive development within the SDGs' "leave no one behind" agenda; twin-track mainstreaming |

CBR thus evolved from a **narrow, service-delivery, medical-model** intervention into a **broad, rights-based, community-development and inclusion** strategy — the single most important narrative in this answer.

### 4. Principles of CBR

Drawn from the UNCRPD and WHO Guidelines, CBR is governed by:

- **Inclusion** — of persons with disabilities in all community activities and mainstream services.
- **Participation** — of persons with disabilities and their families in decisions affecting them ("Nothing about us without us").
- **Sustainability** — programmes owned and maintained by communities and integrated into local systems.
- **Empowerment** — building the capacity and voice of persons with disabilities and DPOs.
- **Self-advocacy and rights** — realising the UNCRPD rights.
- **Non-discrimination, accessibility, and equalisation of opportunities.**
- **Multisectoral collaboration** — across health, education, livelihood, and social sectors.

### 5. CBR as a Strategy Within Community Development

CBR is deliberately positioned *inside* general community development because disability and poverty are mutually reinforcing (the **disability–poverty cycle**): disability increases the risk of poverty (exclusion from education, work, and services), and poverty increases the risk of disability (malnutrition, unsafe environments, poor healthcare). CBR breaks this cycle by:

- Addressing **livelihood** (skills training, self-employment, microfinance, SHGs) alongside health.
- Ensuring **inclusive education** and **social participation** (marriage, family, recreation, culture).
- Building **empowerment** so persons with disabilities become agents of their own and their community's development.
- **Mainstreaming** disability into all local development (water, roads, schools, panchayat) — the twin-track approach.

This distinguishes CBR from institution-based rehabilitation: it is community-owned, low-cost, culturally appropriate, reaches the un-reached, and treats persons with disabilities as **rights-holders and contributors**, not passive patients.

### 6. Roles of Stakeholders (including the Physiotherapist)

- **Persons with disabilities & DPOs** — lead, advocate, and drive the programme.
- **Family and community** — provide day-to-day support and inclusion.
- **CBR workers/mid-level rehabilitation workers** — community-level generalists trained in basic rehabilitation, identification, and linkage.
- **Physiotherapist/rehabilitation professionals** — provide **specialist assessment, prescription, supervision, training, and referral support**; supervise and mentor CBR workers and caregivers; contribute to programme design and the "medical care, rehabilitation, assistive devices" elements of the Health component while advocating across the whole matrix.
- **Government (health, education, social welfare, panchayat)** and **NGOs** — enable, fund, and integrate.

### 7. From CBR to Community-Based Inclusive Development (CBID)

**CBID** is the current evolution (from ~2015) that reframes CBR as a route to **disability-inclusive community development** aligned with the UNCRPD and the SDGs. It shifts emphasis from *rehabilitation of the individual* to *transformation of the community* so that it includes everyone by design — combining **disability-specific support** with **mainstreaming disability across all development sectors** (the twin-track approach). CBID centres the leadership of persons with disabilities and their organisations and embeds inclusion in local governance, making "leave no one behind" operational at community level.

<div class="callout callout-india">

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

- **Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016** — replaced the 1995 Act, recognises **21 disabilities**, mandates accessibility, inclusive education, employment reservation, and community-based programmes, aligning Indian law with the UNCRPD (India ratified UNCRPD in 2007).
- **National Trust Act (1999)** — for autism, cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, and multiple disabilities; runs community-inclusion schemes.
- **Deendayal Disabled Rehabilitation Scheme (DDRS)** — the flagship central scheme funding NGOs to run community-based rehabilitation and services for persons with disabilities.
- **Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) Act, 1992** — regulates training and registration of rehabilitation professionals (including community-based rehabilitation courses) and maintains the Central Rehabilitation Register.
- **DEPwD (Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities)** schemes — **ADIP** (assistive devices), **Accessible India Campaign (Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan)**, **DDRS**, and **Unique Disability ID (UDID)**.
- **NGO/CBR models:** Mobility India (Bengaluru), CBR Forum, Basic Needs India, SEVA-in-Action, Amar Seva Sangam (Tamil Nadu — a globally recognised CBID model), and Kudumbashree's BUDS/community rehabilitation (Kerala).
- **Panchayati Raj + NHM/RBSK** provide integration points — RBSK screens and links children with the "4Ds" (including disabilities) to District Early Intervention Centres (DEICs).
</div>

<div class="callout callout-advance">

**🔬 Recent Advances (2020–2026)**

- **WHO World Report on Disability (2011) → Global Report on Health Equity for Persons with Disabilities (2022)** re-centres disability inclusion, estimating ~1.3 billion people (16% of the world) experience significant disability, and calls for health-system inclusion.
- **WHO "Rehabilitation 2030" initiative and the Package of Interventions for Rehabilitation (PIR, 2023)** integrate rehabilitation into health systems and PHC — directly supporting community rehabilitation scale-up.
- **CBID mainstreaming** within the SDGs and the **UN Disability Inclusion Strategy (2019)**; growth of **inclusive disaster risk reduction** and inclusive humanitarian action.
- **Tele-rehabilitation and mHealth CBR:** digital caregiver training, tele-supervision of CBR workers, and app-based screening extend specialist reach into communities (accelerated by COVID-19).
- **Assistive technology:** WHO **GATE initiative / Priority Assistive Products List** and the **Global Report on Assistive Technology (2022)** promote community-level AT provision — a key CBR Health element.
</div>

### Exam Key Points (rapid revision)

- **CBR (WHO, 1978)** = "a strategy within general community development for rehabilitation, equalisation of opportunities, poverty reduction and social inclusion of all people with disabilities" (ILO/UNESCO/WHO, 2004).
- CBR is developmental and rights-based, not merely medical — implemented through the combined efforts of persons with disabilities themselves, families, communities, and services.
- Underpinned by the shift from **medical → social → biopsychosocial (ICF) → human-rights (UNCRPD, 2006)** models.
- Organised by the **WHO CBR Matrix (2010): Health, Education, Livelihood, Social, Empowerment** (5×5).
- Breaks the **disability–poverty cycle** via the **twin-track** approach (disability-specific + mainstreaming).
- Principles: inclusion, participation, sustainability, empowerment, self-advocacy, non-discrimination, multisectoral collaboration ("Nothing about us without us").
- Evolved to **CBID** — disability-inclusive community development within the SDGs.
- **India:** RPwD Act 2016 (21 disabilities), National Trust Act 1999, DDRS, RCI Act 1992, ADIP, Sugamya Bharat, UDID; models — Mobility India, Amar Seva Sangam, Kudumbashree.
