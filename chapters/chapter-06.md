# Chapter 6 — Assistive Technology and Products

*MPT Community Physiotherapy · Long-Answer Model Notes*

Assistive technology (AT) — the systems, products, devices and related services that maintain or improve an individual's functioning and independence — is now recognised by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a precondition for realising the Sustainable Development Goals, the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) and the goal of Universal Health Coverage. The landmark **WHO–UNICEF Global Report on Assistive Technology (GReAT), 2022** estimated that more than **2.5 billion people** need at least one assistive product today, a figure projected to exceed **3.5 billion by 2050**, yet in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) fewer than 3–10% of those who need AT can access it. India, with the world's largest population and an ageing demographic superimposed on a high burden of non-communicable disease, injury and congenital impairment, sits at the centre of this "assistive technology gap". For the community physiotherapist, AT is not a peripheral add-on but a core rehabilitative competency: prescription, fitting, training and follow-up of wheelchairs, orthoses, prostheses, mobility aids, seating systems and low-vision/hearing products are daily clinical acts that determine whether a person participates in family, school, work and society.

This chapter provides postgraduate physiotherapists with a comprehensive, exam-ready treatment of AT anchored in the WHO **GATE (Global Cooperation on Assistive Technology)** initiative and its 5P framework (People, Policy, Products, Provision, Personnel), the **Priority Assistive Products List (APL, 2016)**, the **WHO eight-step wheelchair service delivery model** and the WSTP/WSTP-Intermediate/TAP training packages, the **ATscale/ATA-2030** partnership, **universal design (UD)** principles and the **International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)**. It answers eight 20-mark long-answer questions:

- **Qa.** Use of Assistive Technology Across the Lifespan
- **Qb.** Integrating Universal Design in Assistive Technology Products
- **Qc.** Adapting WHO's Eight-Step Wheelchair Service Provision for Assistive Technology Prescription
- **Qd.** WHO Priority Assistive Products List (APL) and National List of Essential Assistive Products
- **Qe.** Guidelines for Prescription and Training of Orthosis and Prosthesis
- **Qf.** Guidelines for Adapted Seating Systems

## Qa. Discuss the use of assistive technology across the lifespan, integrating the WHO GATE framework and the ICF. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Assistive technology (AT)** is defined by WHO as "the application of organised knowledge and skills related to assistive products, including systems and services." An **assistive product (AP)** is "any external product (including devices, equipment, instruments or software), especially produced or generally available, whose primary purpose is to maintain or improve an individual's functioning and independence, and thereby promote their well-being" (WHO–UNICEF GReAT, 2022). AT therefore encompasses both the *product* (e.g., a wheelchair, hearing aid, spectacles, prosthesis, communication board) and the *service* (assessment, prescription, fitting, user training, maintenance and follow-up) — the two are inseparable, and a product without competent service is often abandoned.

The concept of "**across the lifespan**" recognises that the *type*, *purpose* and *provision context* of AT change as an individual moves through developmental stages — infancy and early childhood, school age, adolescence, working adulthood, and older age — and that need is dynamic: it fluctuates with growth, disease trajectory, functional decline and changing life roles. A lifespan lens reframes AT away from a one-off "device dispensed" toward a **continuum of provision** requiring re-assessment, re-fitting and transition support.

Key definitional anchors for this answer:

- **WHO GATE** (Global Cooperation on Assistive Technology, launched 2014): the flagship WHO initiative to improve access to high-quality, affordable AT globally, structured around **five interlinked domains (the "5 Ps"): People, Policy, Products, Provision and Personnel.**
- **ICF** (International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health, WHO 2001): AT is coded as an **environmental factor (e1 products and technology)** that acts as a *facilitator* modifying the relationship between body function/structure impairment, activity limitation and participation restriction.
- **Universal Health Coverage (UHC):** AT is a health service that must be available without financial hardship; GReAT positions AT within UHC and the CRPD (Articles 20, 26, 32).

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

Three frameworks structure lifespan AT reasoning.

**(a) The ICF biopsychosocial model.** AT does not "cure" impairment; it modifies the interaction between the person and the environment. A child with cerebral palsy has a body-structure impairment; a postural seating system (AP) is an environmental facilitator that enables the *activity* of sitting and the *participation* of classroom learning. The same logic applies to an older adult whose hearing aid converts a communication-participation restriction into social engagement. ICF makes the *outcome* of AT — participation — the measure of success, not mere possession of a device.

**(b) The WHO GATE 5P framework** operationalises system readiness at every life stage:

| GATE domain | Core question | Lifespan illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **People** | Who needs AT and are users/carers engaged? | User-centred goals differ for a toddler (play), a student (education), a worker (employment), an elder (safe mobility) |
| **Policy** | Are laws, financing and inclusion in UHC in place? | Early-intervention entitlements; school inclusion law; disability pension; geriatric care policy |
| **Products** | Are appropriate, affordable, quality products available? | Growth-adjustable paediatric wheelchairs; modular prostheses; low-vision aids; fall-prevention products |
| **Provision** | Is there a service pathway (assess–fit–train–follow-up)? | Multidisciplinary early-intervention teams; community rehab; home-based geriatric provision |
| **Personnel** | Is the trained workforce adequate? | Paediatric therapists, prosthetists/orthotists, audiologists, community rehab workers, geriatric physios |

**(c) The Human Activity Assistive Technology (HAAT) model** (Cook & Polgar) frames every AT interaction as the interplay of the **Human**, the **Activity**, the **Assistive Technology** and the **Context**. Because all four elements change across the lifespan — the human grows and ages, activities and roles shift, contexts move from home to school to workplace to care setting — the HAAT model justifies periodic re-assessment as a clinical necessity, not an optional extra. The related **Matching Person and Technology (MPT)** model adds the psychosocial dimension of device acceptance, which is strongly age-dependent (e.g., adolescent stigma sensitivity).

### 3. Assistive Technology in Infancy and Early Childhood (0–5 years)

Early childhood is a period of rapid neuroplasticity where AT supports developmental trajectories rather than compensating for fixed loss. Priorities are **postural management, mobility, communication and sensory access**.

- **Postural/seating and standing:** corner seats, adaptive chairs, standing frames and supportive car seats manage tone, prevent contracture and hip subluxation, and enable upright play and social interaction. Early powered mobility (from ~18–24 months) is supported by evidence for cognitive and psychosocial benefit.
- **Mobility:** gait trainers, paediatric walkers, and early wheelchairs; crawling devices for spina bifida.
- **Communication (AAC):** picture boards, single-message switches and early augmentative and alternative communication for non-speaking children.
- **Sensory:** hearing aids and cochlear implant processors (critical window for speech-language development — hence universal newborn hearing screening), and low-vision products.

A defining feature is **rapid outgrowth**: children require re-measurement and re-provision every 6–18 months, demanding modular/adjustable products and family-centred training. Parents and carers are co-prescribers.

### 4. Assistive Technology in School Age and Adolescence (6–18 years)

The dominant participation domain shifts to **education, peer relationships and emerging independence**.

- **Educational AT:** height-adjustable desks, slant boards, adapted writing aids, screen readers, magnification software, Braille displays, FM/hearing-loop systems in classrooms, and computer access (switches, eye-gaze).
- **Mobility and self-care:** self-propelled and powered wheelchairs sized for independence and community travel; toileting and dressing aids to reduce carer dependence.
- **Psychosocial dimension:** adolescence introduces acute **stigma and body-image concerns**; device abandonment peaks when products are conspicuous or "medicalised". Aesthetics, personalisation and peer normalisation (see Qh) become clinically important. Transition planning toward vocational goals begins.

### 5. Assistive Technology in Working-Age Adulthood (19–64 years)

Goals centre on **employment, independent living, parenting and community participation**. Need arises congenitally, from acquired disability (spinal cord injury, stroke, amputation, multiple sclerosis) or from progressive disease.

- **Vocational AT:** workplace ergonomic adaptations, computer access technology, standing wheelchairs, and job-specific orthoses/prostheses (e.g., activity-specific or myoelectric upper-limb prostheses).
- **Mobility and ADL:** lightweight/active wheelchairs, prostheses, orthoses, and home modifications (grab rails, ramps).
- **Reproductive and parenting roles**, driving adaptations, and continence products sustain full adult participation. Reassessment follows changes in occupation, disease progression, or weight change affecting fit.

### 6. Assistive Technology in Older Age (65+ years)

Ageing populations drive the largest projected growth in AT demand. Need is often **multiple, concurrent and progressive** (age-related hearing and vision loss, osteoarthritis, falls, dementia, incontinence).

- **Mobility and falls prevention:** walking sticks, rollators/wheeled walkers, wheelchairs, grab rails, raised toilet seats, and anti-slip products; falls are a leading cause of injury-related disability in elders.
- **Sensory:** hearing aids and spectacles/low-vision aids — the two most prevalent AT needs worldwide.
- **Cognition and safety:** memory aids, medication reminders, personal emergency response/alarm systems, and smart-home/telecare products for people with dementia.
- **Continence and pressure care:** absorbent products, pressure-redistribution cushions and mattresses.

The clinical challenge is **poly-AT integration** (ensuring a walker, hearing aid and spectacles work together for a frail elder) and provision at home through community and geriatric physiotherapy.

*Figure: A lifespan "staircase" can be visualised as five ascending steps (early childhood → school → adolescence → adulthood → older age); at each step the participation domain (play → education → peer identity → work → safe independence) is written above the step, and the AT categories (postural/mobility/AAC → educational/computer access → discreet mobility → vocational/prosthetic → falls/sensory/cognitive) are written on each tread — the whole staircase resting on a foundation labelled "GATE 5Ps".*

### 7. Cross-Cutting Principles for Lifespan Provision

- **Continuity and transitions:** deliberate handover between paediatric and adult services prevents "transition cliffs" where provision lapses at age 18.
- **Re-assessment schedule** proportionate to change (frequent in childhood and in progressive disease, event-triggered in stable adulthood).
- **User- and family-centred goal setting** using ICF participation goals.
- **Appropriateness:** the product must fit the person, the environment and the resources available (WHO's four criteria: appropriate, affordable, accessible, adaptable).
- **Follow-up and maintenance** to prevent abandonment (documented abandonment rates of 30% for many devices).

## Qb. Explain the principles of Universal Design and discuss how they can be integrated into the design and provision of assistive technology products. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

**Universal Design (UD)** is defined by Ronald Mace and the Center for Universal Design (North Carolina State University) as "the design of products and environments to be usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation or specialised design." The CRPD (Article 2) adopts the same definition and adds a crucial rider: "'Universal design' shall not exclude assistive devices for particular groups of persons with disabilities where this is needed." UD is thus a *design philosophy* that seeks to build inclusion into mainstream products from the outset, rather than retrofitting accessibility.

Related but distinct concepts must be separated:

- **Assistive technology (AT):** products *specifically* produced or selected to maintain/improve the functioning of an individual with a functional limitation (WHO). AT is, by design, targeted rather than universal.
- **Design for All / Inclusive Design:** European and British near-synonyms for UD, emphasising a *process* that considers the full diversity of users.
- **Accessible design:** meeting minimum accessibility standards/codes (often the legal floor).

The seemingly paradoxical relationship — UD aims to make *specialised* AT less necessary, yet AT for particular groups remains legitimate — is the intellectual core of this question. The two are complementary points on a **continuum of design responses to human diversity**: UD reduces the *volume* of specialised need; AT meets the *residual* individualised need that no mainstream product can economically cover.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

**The Seven Principles of Universal Design** (Center for Universal Design, 1997) are the canonical framework:

| # | Principle | Definition | AT application example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Equitable use** | Useful and marketable to people with diverse abilities; same/equivalent means of use for all | A wheelchair that fits into mainstream public transport without a separate "disabled" route |
| 2 | **Flexibility in use** | Accommodates a wide range of preferences and abilities | Prosthesis with adjustable alignment; software with adjustable input methods (switch, voice, touch) |
| 3 | **Simple and intuitive use** | Easy to understand regardless of experience, knowledge or language | A hearing aid with one clearly marked control; icon-based AAC |
| 4 | **Perceptible information** | Communicates necessary information effectively regardless of sensory ability | Multi-modal feedback (visual + auditory + tactile) on a powered wheelchair joystick or a device charger |
| 5 | **Tolerance for error** | Minimises hazards of accidental or unintended actions | Wheelchair anti-tip and dynamic stability; medication reminder with confirmation |
| 6 | **Low physical effort** | Used efficiently and comfortably with minimum fatigue | Lightweight active wheelchair; power-assist wheels; low-force switches |
| 7 | **Size and space for approach and use** | Appropriate size and space provided regardless of body size, posture or mobility | Adjustable seating for varied anthropometry; reachable controls |

Two supporting frameworks extend UD into AT:

- **ICF:** UD improves the *environmental factors (e)* that surround the person, reducing the "gap" that AT must bridge. A universally designed building (e155 – design of private buildings) lowers the specialised mobility AT a wheelchair user needs.
- **The "curb-cut effect":** features designed for people with disability (kerb ramps, captions, voice interfaces) benefit the whole population — the economic and social rationale for embedding UD in mainstream products and, increasingly, in AT itself so that AT is not stigmatising or single-use.

### 3. Why Integrate UD into AT? The Rationale

- **Reduces stigma and abandonment.** Products that look mainstream (a hearing aid resembling an earbud, a "cool" prosthetic cover) improve acceptance, particularly for adolescents (link to Qh aesthetics).
- **Widens the user base and lowers cost.** UD-informed AT that serves diverse abilities enjoys larger markets and economies of scale — central to ATscale market-shaping.
- **Improves usability and safety** across the ability spectrum, reducing training burden and error.
- **Future-proofs for changing ability.** A universally designed product accommodates the user's own ageing or disease progression without replacement.
- **Aligns with rights and law** (CRPD Article 4 obliges states to promote UD; the RPwD Act mandates accessibility).

### 4. Strategies for Integrating UD into AT Products

**(a) User-centred and participatory (co-design) processes.** Involve people with disabilities as co-designers from concept to evaluation ("nothing about us without us"). Iterative usability testing with diverse users operationalises Principles 3, 4 and 5.

**(b) Modularity, adjustability and customisation.** Adjustable seat width/depth, tool-free adaptation, and modular components let one platform serve many bodies (Principles 2 and 7) — vital across the lifespan and for growth.

**(c) Multi-modal interfaces.** Provide redundant input (switch/voice/touch/eye-gaze) and output (visual/auditory/tactile) so a single product suits varied sensory and motor profiles (Principles 2, 4).

**(d) Mainstream-technology convergence.** Smartphones and tablets are inherently UD platforms; using them as AT (screen readers, magnification, AAC apps, hearing-aid control) delivers powerful, discreet, low-cost assistance. This "mainstreaming" is one of the most important 21st-century AT trends.

**(e) Aesthetics and personalisation.** Offer colours, covers and forms that let users express identity, directly reducing stigma (Principle 1: equitable, non-stigmatising use).

**(f) Standards and guidelines.** Apply ISO 9241 (usability/ergonomics of human–system interaction), ISO/IEC accessibility standards, WCAG for digital AT, and WHO product specifications for quality and safety.

### 5. Tensions and Limits of UD for AT

UD is powerful but not a panacea:

- **Residual individualised need.** Severe or complex impairments (e.g., custom sockets for a residual limb, individually moulded seating for severe deformity) cannot be met by universal products — hence the CRPD proviso protecting specialised AT.
- **Cost and complexity trade-offs.** Building in maximal flexibility can raise price or complexity, conflicting with affordability (a core WHO criterion) in LMICs.
- **"Average user" trap.** Poorly executed UD designs to a mythical average and excludes the extremes; genuine UD requires designing to the *edges* (extreme-user/inclusive design).
- **UD complements, does not replace, AT provision services** (assessment, fitting, training, follow-up remain essential).

The overlap — labelled "Universally designed AT / mainstream tech used as AT (e.g., smartphones, ergonomic tools)" — is the growing zone this question targets; the non-overlapping AT crescent represents irreducible specialised need protected by CRPD Article 2.*

## Qc. Describe the WHO eight-step wheelchair service provision model and discuss how it can be adapted as a generic framework for assistive technology prescription. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

The **WHO eight-step wheelchair service delivery model** was published in the *Guidelines on the Provision of Manual Wheelchairs in Less Resourced Settings* (WHO, 2008) and operationalised through the **Wheelchair Service Training Package (WSTP – Basic level, 2012; Intermediate level, 2013)** and later the **Training in Packages / TAP (Training of Trainers, and the Wheelchair Service Training Package for Managers and Stakeholders)**. It provides a standardised, quality-assured **service pathway** that converts a wheelchair from a mere product into an appropriately prescribed, fitted and supported assistive intervention.

A **service delivery model** is the structured sequence of steps by which an assistive product is matched to a user, provided, and supported over time. The wheelchair model's importance is that it is the most fully developed, evidence-informed and internationally taught AT service framework, and its logic — assessment → prescription → provision → training → follow-up — is **generalisable to virtually all assistive products** (prostheses, orthoses, hearing aids, low-vision devices, seating). This question requires both an accurate account of the eight steps *and* a reasoned adaptation to AT prescription generally.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

The model sits within the **WHO GATE 5P framework** as the "**Provision**" pillar, and is designed so that appropriately trained "**Personnel**" match quality "**Products**" to "**People**", enabled by "**Policy**". It embodies four WHO principles of appropriate AT — **appropriate, affordable, accessible, adaptable** — and is user-centred: the user (and family) participate at every step. Outcomes are framed in **ICF** terms (mobility, activity and participation), not merely device provision. The overall cycle mirrors generic quality-improvement logic (assess–plan–do–review) and the **Matching Person and Technology** emphasis on user fit and follow-up to prevent abandonment.

### 3. The Eight Steps of the WHO Wheelchair Service Delivery Model

| Step | Name | Core activities | Key output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Referral and appointment** | Identify and refer users to the service; register and schedule | User enters the service pathway |
| 2 | **Assessment** | Interview physical, lifestyle and environmental needs; physical assessment (posture, pressure risk, range, strength, deformity, transfers); goal setting | Comprehensive user profile and goals |
| 3 | **Prescription (selection)** | Match user needs to an appropriate product; specify wheelchair type, size and features, and postural support/pressure-relief cushion | Individualised prescription |
| 4 | **Funding and ordering** | Secure funding; order the specified product and components | Product procured |
| 5 | **Product preparation** | Assemble/prepare and check the product is ready and safe before fitting | Ready, safe product |
| 6 | **Fitting** | Adjust to fit the user; check posture, pressure, propulsion and safety; make modifications | Correctly fitted product |
| 7 | **User training** | Train user and carer in safe use, propulsion/transfers, pressure relief, maintenance and problem-solving | Competent, safe user |
| 8 | **Follow-up, maintenance and repairs** | Review fit, function and skin/health; repair/maintain; re-assess as needs change | Sustained, safe long-term use |

Two features are essential to emphasise in an exam: (i) the steps are **cyclical, not linear** — follow-up (step 8) feeds back into re-assessment (step 2) as the user's needs, weight, environment or condition change; and (ii) **user training and follow-up (steps 7–8) are where most services fail**, and where abandonment and pressure-injury risk are highest. Skipping the physical assessment or the pressure/postural prescription can be dangerous (pressure injuries, deformity, injury from an unsafe product).

### 4. Adapting the Eight-Step Model as a Generic AT Prescription Framework

The strength of the model is its **transferability**. Below, each step is generalised to any assistive product, with device-specific illustrations.

| Wheelchair step | Generic AT step | Prosthetics/orthotics | Hearing/vision | Seating/AAC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Referral & appointment | Identify need, refer, register | Referral post-amputation/for orthosis | Screening (e.g., NPPCD, vision camp) referral | Referral from school/rehab team |
| Assessment | Holistic ICF assessment + goals | Residual limb, gait, function, lifestyle | Audiometry / visual acuity + function | Postural/motor/communication assessment |
| Prescription/selection | Match product to needs | Prosthesis/orthosis type, components | Hearing-aid type/gain; low-vision aid | Seating/AAC system specification |
| Funding & ordering | Secure funding, procure | ADIP/ALIMCO order | Device order | Custom seating order |
| Product preparation | Fabricate/prepare & safety check | Cast, fabricate socket/orthosis | Ear-mould/programming | Mould/assemble seating |
| Fitting | Fit and adjust | Socket fit, alignment | Real-ear verification, fitting | Postural fit and pressure check |
| User training | Train user/carer | Donning/doffing, gait, skin care | Device use, listening/reading strategies | Access method, maintenance |
| Follow-up | Review, maintain, re-assess | Socket refit, component service | Re-programming, re-assessment | Growth/tone re-fitting |

**Adaptations required for a truly generic framework:**

- **Discipline-specific assessment competencies** (audiometry, gait analysis, low-vision assessment) replace the wheelchair-specific physical assessment, but the *structure* (impairment + activity + participation + environment) is constant.
- **Fabrication step is variable:** off-the-shelf products (many hearing aids, spectacles) compress steps 5–6, whereas custom products (sockets, moulded seating) expand them.
- **Follow-up interval** must be tuned to the product and user (frequent for growing children and progressive disease; event-triggered for stable adults).
- **Multidisciplinary teamwork** and clear referral pathways are essential where several products interact.
- **Personnel training** analogous to WSTP is required for each product class (WHO's TAP and the analogous prosthetics/orthotics competency standards).

### 5. Quality, Training and System Requirements

Effective provision needs: trained and competency-assessed personnel (WSTP basic/intermediate, Training of Trainers); reliable supply of quality products and spares; funding mechanisms; referral integration with primary care and community rehabilitation; documentation and outcome measurement; and a maintenance/repair infrastructure. WHO frames these as the "Provision" and "Personnel" pillars; without them the eight steps cannot function.

## Qd. Describe the WHO Priority Assistive Products List (APL) and discuss the rationale, process and significance of developing a National List of Essential/Priority Assistive Products. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

The **WHO Priority Assistive Products List (APL)** was published in 2016 as the first deliverable of the GATE initiative. It is a list of **50 priority assistive products** selected on the basis of widespread need and impact on a person's life — explicitly modelled on the **WHO Model List of Essential Medicines**. The APL is not intended as a restrictive or exhaustive catalogue but as a **minimum, priority set** that every country should make available and affordable to meet the most common AT needs, as a first step toward broader provision.

Definitions to anchor the answer:

- **Priority assistive products:** the assistive products needed by the largest number of people, that have the greatest impact on functioning and participation, and that a country should therefore prioritise for availability and financing.
- **National Priority/Essential Assistive Products List (NPAPL/APL):** a country's own adaptation of the WHO APL to national epidemiology, resources, health-system structure and financing — the practical instrument for embedding AT in Universal Health Coverage.
- **Essential (vs comprehensive) provision:** the "essential list" concept ensures a guaranteed minimum for all, analogous to essential medicines and essential diagnostics.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

The APL operationalises the **"Products" pillar** of the **GATE 5P framework**, but its power is systemic: by defining *what* should be available, it drives **Policy** (financing, inclusion in benefit packages), **Provision** (service pathways for those products) and **Personnel** (the workforce to prescribe them). It aligns AT with **Universal Health Coverage** and the **CRPD** (Articles 20, 26, 32), and with the **Sustainable Development Goals**. The methodology mirrors the **Essential Medicines** paradigm: prioritise by public-health need, impact, and feasibility, then guarantee access. The APL was developed through a **Delphi consensus** of experts and a global survey of users, ensuring a demand-driven list.

### 3. Structure and Content of the WHO APL (50 products)

The 50 products span multiple functional domains. Representative examples by domain:

| Functional domain | Representative priority products |
|---|---|
| **Mobility** | Manual wheelchairs (basic, with postural support), tricycles, walking frames/rollators, walking sticks/crutches, canes, standing frames |
| **Vision** | Spectacles (near, distance), magnifiers (optical/digital), Braille displays/writing equipment, screen readers, white canes |
| **Hearing** | Hearing aids (and batteries), personal/assistive listening devices |
| **Communication (AAC)** | Communication boards/books, speech-generating (recorded/keyboard-based) devices, video communication devices |
| **Cognition** | Time-management products, memory aids, medication management/simple day-planners |
| **Self-care & environment** | Absorbent continence products, pressure-relief cushions and mattresses, commodes/toilet/shower chairs, grab rails, standing/therapeutic footwear |
| **Prosthetics & orthotics** | Lower-limb prostheses, orthoses (upper/lower limb, spinal), therapeutic/protective footwear, club-foot braces |

The list deliberately balances high-technology (screen readers, hearing aids) with simple, low-cost, high-impact products (spectacles, canes, continence pads, pressure cushions), reflecting global need where the commonest unmet needs are for spectacles, hearing aids and mobility products.

### 4. Rationale for a National List of Priority Assistive Products

- **Contextualisation:** national epidemiology (e.g., high injury/amputation burden, ageing, congenital conditions), climate, terrain and culture shape which products matter most; the WHO APL is a template, not a prescription.
- **Financing and UHC integration:** a national list defines the benefit package the government commits to fund/subsidise, making entitlement concrete and budgetable.
- **Procurement and market-shaping:** a defined list enables pooled procurement, quality standards and price negotiation (ATscale market-shaping), improving affordability and quality.
- **Provision and workforce planning:** knowing the priority products lets a country plan service pathways (per Qc) and train the requisite personnel.
- **Equity and accountability:** a published list is a rights instrument — citizens and civil society can hold the system accountable for guaranteed minimum access.
- **Standardisation:** reduces fragmentation across schemes and ensures quality/safety specifications.

### 5. Process of Developing a National List (WHO-recommended steps)

1. **Establish need and evidence:** conduct a national AT needs assessment (e.g., the **rATA – rapid Assistive Technology Assessment survey**) to quantify prevalence and unmet need by product.
2. **Multi-stakeholder committee:** convene government (health, social welfare, education), user organisations (DPOs), clinicians (physiotherapists, P&O, audiology, ophthalmology), industry and academia — "nothing about us without us".
3. **Adapt the WHO APL:** review the 50 products against national epidemiology, resources and existing schemes; add or reprioritise (e.g., include products for locally prevalent conditions).
4. **Define specifications and standards:** quality, safety and technical specifications for each listed product.
5. **Cost and financing plan:** integrate into the national health benefit package/insurance; identify procurement mechanism.
6. **Service and workforce mapping:** align provision pathways and personnel training with the list.
7. **Publish, implement, monitor and revise:** periodic review as needs, evidence and technology evolve.

### 6. Significance and Challenges

**Significance:** the APL and national lists are the single most influential policy lever for closing the AT gap — they convert the abstract right to AT into a defined, financed, procurable and accountable set of guarantees, and they anchor AT firmly within health systems and UHC.

**Challenges:** securing sustainable financing; ensuring quality (avoiding cheap, unsafe products); building the provision/personnel pathway so listed products are not merely "available on paper"; keeping the list current with technology; and avoiding a list so minimal it excludes those with complex needs.

## Qe. Discuss the guidelines for the prescription and training of orthoses and prostheses. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

An **orthosis** is defined (ISO 8549) as "an externally applied device used to modify the structural and functional characteristics of the neuromuscular and skeletal system." A **prosthesis** is "an externally applied device used to replace wholly, or in part, an absent or deficient limb segment." The field is termed **prosthetics and orthotics (P&O)**, and the practitioner a **prosthetist/orthotist**. Physiotherapists are integral members of the P&O team, responsible for pre- and post-fitting rehabilitation, gait and functional training, and long-term follow-up.

WHO, jointly with USAID and ISPO (International Society for Prosthetics and Orthotics), published the **WHO Standards for Prosthetics and Orthotics (2017)** — the primary international guideline covering the four pillars of **People, Products, Personnel and Provision** for P&O services. Prescription and training of orthoses and prostheses must follow a **structured, team-based, evidence-informed pathway** to ensure a device that is appropriate, well-fitting, safe, functional and used.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

P&O provision is framed by:

- **ICF:** the device is an environmental facilitator (e1151 assistive products for personal mobility) that restores body structure (limb replacement) or modifies body function (control of tone, alignment, protection), enabling activity (walking, standing, hand function) and participation (work, mobility, social roles). Goal-setting is participation-focused.
- **WHO/ISPO Standards (2017)** — the "4 pillars" (People-centred, Products, Personnel, Provision) — provide the quality architecture.
- **Biomechanical principles:** orthotic design applies three-point pressure systems, ground-reaction forces and lever mechanics; prosthetic prescription matches componentry (foot, knee, socket, suspension) to the user's functional level (e.g., **Medicare Functional Classification Level / K-levels K0–K4**).
- **Team model:** prosthetist/orthotist + physiotherapist + physician (physiatrist/surgeon) + occupational therapist + the user, working collaboratively.

### 3. General Guidelines for Prescription

Prescription follows the **service pathway analogous to the WHO eight steps (Qc)**: referral → assessment → prescription → fabrication → fitting → training → follow-up. Prescription proper (selecting the device and components) rests on:

- **Comprehensive assessment:** medical history and diagnosis; skin and residual-limb/joint condition; range of motion, strength, tone, sensation, deformity, oedema, vascularity; pain; cognitive status; functional level and goals; vocational/social context and environment.
- **Matching componentry to functional level and goals:** e.g., K-levels guide prosthetic foot/knee selection; orthosis type is chosen by biomechanical need (stabilise, support, offload, correct, assist/substitute for weak muscles).
- **User-centred goal setting and shared decision-making**, considering weight, activity, cognition, cosmetic needs, resources and maintenance capacity.
- **Appropriateness** (WHO's four criteria) and the **least restrictive** device that meets the goal.
- **Documentation** of prescription rationale.

### 4. Guidelines Specific to Prostheses

**(a) Pre-prosthetic phase (physiotherapy-led):** residual-limb care and desensitisation; oedema control (elastic wrapping/shrinker/rigid dressing); wound and skin management; contracture prevention (positioning, ROM — especially hip/knee extension for lower limb); strengthening (core, proximal muscles, sound limb); cardiovascular conditioning; balance and transfers; psychological support and realistic expectation setting; early mobility (with mobility aids). Prosthetic candidacy is assessed (vascular status, cognition, motivation, comorbidity).

**(b) Prescription of components:** socket design and suspension (e.g., total-surface-bearing, suction, pin-lock, vacuum); prosthetic foot (SACH, energy-storing, dynamic-response, microprocessor) and knee (single-axis, polycentric, microprocessor/MPK) matched to K-level; upper-limb prostheses (body-powered, myoelectric, activity-specific) matched to occupational needs.

**(c) Fitting and alignment:** static and dynamic alignment; socket fit and pressure tolerance; suspension security; check socket/trial before definitive fabrication.

**(d) Prosthetic training (physiotherapy-led):**
- Donning/doffing and residual-limb/sock management; skin inspection.
- Progressive **gait training** — weight shift, stance and swing control, cadence, negotiating stairs, ramps, uneven terrain, falls and floor transfers.
- Upper-limb: control training (myoelectric signal training, terminal-device operation), prehension and bimanual task practice.
- Wearing-schedule progression to tolerance; monitoring skin; energy-conservation.

### 5. Guidelines Specific to Orthoses

**(a) Classification and indication:** name by body segment (AFO, KAFO, HKAFO, spinal orthoses, WHO/wrist-hand, cervical) and function (stabilising, corrective, assistive, protective, offloading). Match to biomechanical goal — e.g., AFO for foot-drop (dorsiflexion assist) or for knee control (ground-reaction AFO), club-foot bracing (Ponseti), spinal orthosis for scoliosis or stabilisation, resting hand splint for tone/contracture.

**(b) Prescription principles:** apply three-point force systems; ensure joints of the orthosis align with anatomical joints; allow for growth in children; balance rigidity/control against comfort and function; avoid pressure over bony prominences and vulnerable/insensate skin.

**(c) Fitting:** check alignment, pressure areas, skin tolerance, ease of donning/doffing, and functional effect (gait, posture, hand function); modify as needed.

**(d) Orthotic training (physiotherapy-led):** donning/doffing; wearing schedule (gradual build-up with skin checks — critical in insensate skin, e.g., diabetes, spina bifida); gait/functional training with the orthosis; strengthening and stretching to complement it; carer education.

### 6. Cross-Cutting Guidelines: Training, Follow-up and Safety

| Element | Prostheses | Orthoses |
|---|---|---|
| **Wearing schedule** | Graduated, skin-checked build-up | Graduated, skin-checked (vital if insensate) |
| **Skin care** | Daily inspection of residual limb; manage sweating/breakdown | Inspect pressure areas; adjust for redness >20–30 min |
| **Functional training** | Gait, terrain, transfers, ADL, sport/vocation | Gait/posture/hand function; task-specific practice |
| **Maintenance** | Socket fit changes with volume; component service | Adjust for growth/wear; strap and joint checks |
| **Follow-up** | Refit for limb volume change; component review | Re-fit for growth/deformity change; skin review |
| **Red flags** | Skin breakdown, pain, falls, choke syndrome | Pressure injury, non-tolerance, worsening deformity |

Safety is paramount: **insensate and vascularly compromised skin** (diabetes, spinal cord injury, leprosy) demands meticulous fitting and wearing-schedule discipline to prevent ulceration; falls risk must be managed during gait training; and psychological adjustment and adherence support reduce abandonment.
