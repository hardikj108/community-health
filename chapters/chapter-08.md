# Chapter 8 — Geriatrics

*MPT Community Physiotherapy · Long-Answer Model Notes*

Population ageing is one of the defining public-health transitions of the twenty-first century. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that the number of people aged 60 years and over will rise from about **1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion by 2030 and 2.1 billion by 2050**, with the fastest growth occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In response, the United Nations and WHO declared the **Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030)**, a global collaboration built on four action areas — changing how we think, feel and act towards age; developing age-friendly environments; delivering integrated care; and providing access to long-term care. For India, which is projected to have over **300 million older persons by 2050**, this transition is compressed and superimposed on a persisting burden of communicable disease, malnutrition and a young workforce migrating away from ageing parents. For the community physiotherapist, geriatric rehabilitation is therefore not a narrow clinical sub-speciality but a central instrument for preserving **intrinsic capacity**, **functional ability** and **participation** across a rapidly ageing society.

This chapter provides postgraduate physiotherapists with an exam-ready, evidence-based treatment of community geriatrics, anchored throughout in the **WHO Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030)**, the WHO **Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE)** approach and its construct of **intrinsic capacity**, **Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA)**, the **frailty** models of Fried (phenotype) and Rockwood (deficit accumulation/Clinical Frailty Scale), evidence-based **falls prevention**, **sarcopenia** (EWGSOP2/AWGS), and the **International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)**. It answers five 20-mark long-answer questions:

- **Qa.** Implementation of Healthy Ageing Programs
- **Qb.** Components of Geriatric Care and Rehabilitation
- **Qc.** Developing a Multi-Component Geriatric Rehabilitation Program
- **Qd.** Improving Geriatric Rehabilitation Service Provisions in Healthcare Delivery
- **Qe.** Implementing Geriatric Rehabilitation Programs Across Settings (Inpatient, Outpatient, Specialised Institutions, and Communities)

## Qa. Describe the implementation of healthy ageing programmes in the community. Discuss the conceptual framework, planning steps, delivery models and evaluation, with reference to the WHO Decade of Healthy Ageing and ICOPE. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

Healthy ageing has moved decisively away from a "disease-absence" model towards a **functional-ability** model. The WHO defines **healthy ageing** as *"the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables well-being in older age."* This definition reframes the goal of geriatric services: success is not the elimination of disease (unrealistic in a multimorbid population) but the preservation of what an older person is *able to do and to be*, in interaction with their environment.

Two constructs are central. **Intrinsic capacity** is *"the composite of all the physical and mental capacities that an individual can draw on"* — it spans five domains: **locomotion, cognition, vitality (nutrition/energy), psychological capacity, and sensory (vision and hearing)** capacity. **Functional ability** comprises the health-related attributes that enable people to be and to do what they have reason to value; it is the interaction of intrinsic capacity with the **environment** (physical, social, and the enabling services and attitudes within it). A person with declining intrinsic capacity can still have high functional ability if the environment compensates (e.g., mobility aids, ramps, supportive family).

A **healthy ageing programme** is a planned, population-level or community-level set of interventions designed to optimise trajectories of intrinsic capacity and functional ability across the life course, and to build age-friendly environments. It is preventive and promotive in emphasis, multisectoral in delivery, and equity-oriented.

Related definitions the examiner expects:

- **WHO Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030):** a global, UN-endorsed collaboration to improve the lives of older people, their families and communities through concerted action across four areas.
- **ICOPE (Integrated Care for Older People):** WHO's community-level operational model that translates the intrinsic-capacity concept into screening, assessment and person-centred care pathways delivered primarily in primary care.
- **Active ageing (WHO 2002):** the earlier pillar framework (health, participation, security) that preceded and informs healthy ageing.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

Effective implementation rests on several named frameworks that a postgraduate answer must state explicitly.

**(a) The WHO healthy-ageing / functional-ability framework.** The WHO *World Report on Ageing and Health (2015)* describes three trajectories of intrinsic capacity across older age: a **high and stable** period, a **declining-capacity** period, and a period of **significant loss of capacity**. Programmes map to these: capacity-building and health promotion in the stable phase; reversal/slowing of decline (e.g., ICOPE screening, exercise for early frailty/sarcopenia) in the declining phase; and management of severe loss plus long-term care and dignity in the final phase.

**(b) The four action areas of the Decade of Healthy Ageing.** (i) Combating **ageism**; (ii) creating **age-friendly environments**; (iii) delivering **integrated care** responsive to older people (ICOPE); and (iv) providing access to **long-term care**. Every healthy ageing programme should locate its activities within these areas.

**(c) The life-course perspective.** Intrinsic capacity in old age is the accumulated product of exposures across the whole life. Programmes therefore extend upstream: nutrition, physical activity, tobacco/alcohol control and NCD prevention in mid-life are healthy-ageing interventions.

**(d) Implementation-science and planning models.** Programme *design and roll-out* should draw on established models:

- **PRECEDE-PROCEED** — for diagnosing predisposing, enabling and reinforcing factors and structuring evaluation.
- **RE-AIM** (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) — the dominant framework for evaluating whether a healthy ageing programme actually works at scale and is sustained.
- **Knowledge-to-Action (KTA) cycle** — for adapting global guidance (e.g., ICOPE) to local context.
- **CFIR (Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research)** — for identifying barriers/facilitators across intervention, inner and outer settings.

**(e) The ICF.** Healthy ageing outcomes are naturally expressed in ICF language — body functions/structures (intrinsic capacity), activity (functional ability), participation (well-being), and contextual (environmental/personal) factors — giving physiotherapists a common measurement grammar.

### 3. The ICOPE Approach as the Operational Backbone

ICOPE operationalises healthy ageing in five steps that form a community pathway:

1. **Step 1 — Screen for losses in intrinsic capacity** using a brief, low-tech tool covering the six ICOPE conditions: impaired **cognition**, limited **mobility**, **malnutrition**, **visual** impairment, **hearing** loss, and **depressive symptoms**. This can be done by community health workers or via the **ICOPE app / WHO ICOPE Handbook** tools.
2. **Step 2 — Person-centred assessment** of those who screen positive, in primary care, including social/environmental assessment.
3. **Step 3 — Develop a personalised care plan** with goals, interventions (including exercise, nutrition, cognitive stimulation, sensory correction), and self-management support.
4. **Step 4 — Ensure a referral pathway and monitor** the care plan.
5. **Step 5 — Engage communities and support caregivers.**

Physiotherapists are integral to Steps 2–4 for the **locomotion** and **vitality** domains: administering the **chair-rise test / Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB)**, prescribing progressive resistance and balance training, and leading falls-prevention.

### 4. Steps in Implementing a Community Healthy Ageing Programme

A structured implementation sequence (aligning PRECEDE-PROCEED with WHO guidance):

#### 4.1 Situational analysis and needs assessment

Map demography (proportion 60+, dependency ratio), the epidemiological profile (NCDs, falls, frailty, sensory loss), existing services, human resources, and community assets. Use routine data and rapid surveys; in India the **Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI)** provides district-usable prevalence data.

#### 4.2 Stakeholder engagement and governance

Convene health, social welfare, urban/rural local bodies (panchayats), self-help groups, senior citizens' associations, and NGOs. Establish a coordinating committee and clear roles — essential for the multisectoral nature of healthy ageing.

#### 4.3 Setting objectives and selecting evidence-based interventions

Define SMART objectives against intrinsic-capacity domains. Select interventions with strong evidence: multicomponent **exercise** (strength, balance, aerobic), **falls-prevention** bundles, **nutritional** support and protein optimisation, **cognitive stimulation**, **sensory correction** (cataract surgery, hearing aids), immunisation, and **age-friendly environment** modifications.

#### 4.4 Capacity-building and task-shifting

Train primary-care staff, ASHAs/ANMs, and volunteers in ICOPE screening; train physiotherapists as programme leads for movement interventions. Task-shifting is indispensable given the geriatrician/therapist shortage in LMICs.

#### 4.5 Service delivery and integration

Embed screening into existing primary-care contacts (Ayushman Arogya Mandir/HWC wellness days), run community exercise groups, home visits for the homebound, and tele-links for specialist input.

#### 4.6 Monitoring, evaluation and quality improvement

Apply **RE-AIM** indicators (see table). Use PDSA cycles for iterative improvement.

The following table summarises the implementation phases, key activities and indicators.

| Phase | Key activities | Frameworks/tools | Illustrative indicators |
|-------|---------------|------------------|-------------------------|
| 1. Situational analysis | Demographic & epidemiological mapping; asset mapping | PRECEDE (social/epidemiological diagnosis); LASI data | % population 60+; frailty & falls prevalence |
| 2. Governance & engagement | Multisectoral committee; community mobilisation | CFIR outer setting; Age-friendly Cities network | Stakeholders engaged; policy commitments |
| 3. Objectives & interventions | SMART goals; evidence-based package | WHO ICOPE; Decade action areas | Interventions selected & mapped to IC domains |
| 4. Capacity building | Train PHC staff, ASHAs, volunteers; PT leads | Task-shifting; ICOPE Handbook/app | Staff trained; competency assessed |
| 5. Delivery & integration | Screening at HWCs; exercise groups; home & tele care | ICOPE 5 steps; ICF | Reach (% screened); adoption by sites |
| 6. Monitoring & evaluation | Track outcomes; QI cycles | RE-AIM; PDSA | Effectiveness (IC maintained); Maintenance at 12 mo |

*Figure: A pyramid can be described with a broad base of population-wide health promotion and age-friendly environments, a middle tier of ICOPE community screening and targeted interventions for declining capacity, and a narrow apex of intensive rehabilitation and long-term care for severe capacity loss.*

### 5. Delivery Models

- **Facility-based** healthy ageing clinics/wellness days at primary care.
- **Community/outreach** models: exercise groups in community halls, temples, parks; camp-based screening.
- **Home-based** care for the homebound frail, delivered by trained community workers with PT supervision.
- **Digital/tele-models:** the **ICOPE app**, tele-geriatrics for specialist review, and remote monitoring.
- **Integrated primary-care** model (the WHO-preferred): healthy ageing embedded into existing UHC-oriented primary care rather than a vertical programme.

### 6. Barriers and Enablers

Barriers: **ageism** (in society and health systems), fragmented care, workforce shortage, out-of-pocket costs, weak long-term-care infrastructure, and poor age-disaggregated data. Enablers: political commitment (Decade of Healthy Ageing), task-shifting, community ownership, digital tools, and integration into UHC.

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

India's flagship is the **National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE)**, launched in 2010–11, which establishes **Geriatric Departments and Regional Geriatric Centres** in medical colleges, **dedicated geriatric OPD/beds and physiotherapy units at District Hospitals**, and elderly clinics at CHC/PHC levels, with domiciliary care through sub-centres. Legislatively, the **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007** obliges children/relatives to maintain parents and mandates old-age homes and elder-care provisions in each district — a legal underpinning for long-term care. Recognition schemes such as the **Vayoshreshtha Samman** national awards honour eminent senior citizens and institutions serving them, and the **SAGE (Seniorcare Ageing Growth Engine) initiative and SAGE/Senior Care portal** of the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment promote credible start-ups delivering elder-care products and services. Assistive-device support is provided through the **Rashtriya Vayoshri Yojana (RVY)**, distributing aids and assistive-living devices to BPL senior citizens.

Epidemiologically, the **Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI Wave 1, 2020)** — the largest survey of its kind — documents a high burden of hypertension, diabetes, functional limitation, falls, depression and vision/hearing impairment among the 60+, with marked rural–urban and gender inequities and low healthcare utilisation. India is also an active member of the WHO **Global Network for Age-friendly Cities and Communities**, with pilot age-friendly city work in several states. Together these give the physiotherapist real levers — NPHCE physiotherapy units, HWC screening, RVY devices, and the Senior Citizens Act — on which to build community healthy ageing programmes.

## Qb. Describe the components of geriatric care and rehabilitation. Explain the role of Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment, the interdisciplinary team, and the domains of rehabilitation in optimising function in older adults. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

Geriatric care differs qualitatively from adult care because older people present with **multimorbidity**, **polypharmacy**, atypical disease presentation, the **geriatric giants**, reduced physiological reserve (**homeostenosis**) and a strong interplay between medical, functional, psychological and social factors. **Geriatric rehabilitation** is defined as *"a set of interventions designed to optimise functioning and reduce disability in older individuals with health conditions, in interaction with their environment."* It is inseparable from geriatric *care*, which is the broader clinical, preventive and social management of the older person.

The unifying tool that organises geriatric care is the **Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA)** — *"a multidimensional, interdisciplinary diagnostic and therapeutic process to determine the medical, psychological and functional capabilities of a frail older person in order to develop a coordinated and integrated plan for treatment and long-term follow-up."* Cochrane evidence shows that inpatient CGA increases the likelihood that older people are alive and in their own homes at follow-up.

Key definitions the examiner expects:

- **Geriatric giants ("the 5 I's"):** **I**mmobility, **I**nstability (falls), **I**ncontinence, **I**ntellectual impairment (cognitive/delirium/dementia), **I**atrogenesis; some add impaired vision/hearing and iso­lation.
- **Frailty:** a state of increased vulnerability to stressors due to decline across multiple physiological systems — operationalised by the **Fried phenotype** (≥3 of: unintentional weight loss, weakness/low grip, exhaustion, slow gait, low activity) or the **Rockwood deficit-accumulation/Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS)**.
- **Sarcopenia:** progressive, generalised loss of skeletal muscle mass and function; diagnosed (EWGSOP2/AWGS) via low **strength** (grip/chair-rise), confirmed by low **muscle mass**, with severity by low **physical performance** (gait speed/SPPB).
- **Functional status:** measured as **Basic ADL** (Katz/Barthel) and **Instrumental ADL** (Lawton).

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

**(a) ICF.** Geriatric rehabilitation is best conceptualised through the ICF: a health condition interacts with **body functions/structures** (e.g., muscle power, cognition — the intrinsic-capacity substrate), **activities** (mobility, ADL), **participation** (community, family roles), and **contextual factors** (home environment, caregiver support, attitudes). Rehabilitation intervenes at every level.

**(b) WHO intrinsic capacity / functional ability model** (Q a) — CGA and rehabilitation are the means of maintaining intrinsic capacity and augmenting the environment.

**(c) The disablement pathway and the geriatric "vicious cycles."** Nagi/ICF disablement models explain how impairment (e.g., sarcopenia) → activity limitation (slow gait) → participation restriction (housebound). The **frailty cycle** (sarcopenia → weakness → slow gait → reduced activity → further sarcopenia and undernutrition) is a key target for physiotherapy.

**(d) Person-centred, goal-oriented care.** Rehabilitation goals are set collaboratively (e.g., using **Goal Attainment Scaling**) around what the older person values, not merely impairment scores.

### 3. Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment — the Organising Component

CGA is multidimensional; each domain has assessment tools and therapeutic implications.

| CGA domain | Purpose | Common tools | Rehabilitation/PT implication |
|-----------|---------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| Medical | Diagnose disease, multimorbidity, polypharmacy | History/exam; medication review; comorbidity indices | Manage NCDs; identify red flags, exercise contraindications |
| Functional | Quantify ADL/IADL & mobility | Barthel, Katz, Lawton IADL, SPPB, TUG, gait speed | Set functional goals; task-specific training |
| Physical performance/frailty | Detect frailty & sarcopenia | Fried phenotype, CFS, grip dynamometry, chair-rise | Targeted resistance/balance training, protein |
| Cognitive | Detect dementia/delirium | MMSE, MoCA, clock-draw, CAM (delirium) | Adapt instructions; dual-task/cognitive training |
| Psychological | Detect depression/anxiety | GDS-15, PHQ-9 | Behavioural activation via exercise; referral |
| Nutritional | Detect malnutrition risk | MNA, BMI, weight loss | Coordinate protein/energy support with dietitian |
| Sensory | Vision & hearing | Snellen, whisper/audiometry | Environmental adaptation; falls-risk reduction |
| Social & environmental | Support, finances, home safety | Home hazard checklist, caregiver assessment | Home modification; assistive devices; caregiver training |
| Continence | Bladder/bowel | Bladder diary | Pelvic floor training; toileting programmes |

*Figure: CGA can be described as a wheel with the older person at the hub and the domains as spokes, each feeding into a single integrated, prioritised care plan rather than parallel disease silos.*

### 4. The Interdisciplinary Geriatric Team

Geriatric care is delivered by a coordinated team, ideally with shared goals and joint care-planning:

- **Geriatrician/physician** — medical stabilisation, medication rationalisation.
- **Physiotherapist** — mobility, strength, balance, falls prevention, exercise prescription (central to locomotion and vitality).
- **Occupational therapist** — ADL retraining, home modification, assistive technology, cognitive strategies.
- **Nurse** — care coordination, continence, skin integrity, medication administration.
- **Dietitian** — nutrition and sarcopenia management.
- **Speech-language therapist** — dysphagia, communication.
- **Clinical psychologist / psychiatrist** — mood, cognition, behavioural symptoms.
- **Social worker** — entitlements, placement, caregiver support.
- **Pharmacist** — deprescribing, polypharmacy review.
- **Caregiver / family** — the pivotal partner, especially in India's family-based care.

The **interdisciplinary** (not merely multidisciplinary) model — with shared assessment, joint goals and regular team conferencing — is what produces CGA's benefit.

### 5. Domains / Components of Geriatric Rehabilitation

#### 5.1 Physical (musculoskeletal & mobility) rehabilitation

The core physiotherapy component: **progressive resistance training** (to reverse sarcopenia), **balance training** (falls prevention), **aerobic/endurance** work, **flexibility**, **gait and transfer training**, and **prescription of mobility aids**. Interventions are dosed and progressed, adapted for frailty.

#### 5.2 Falls prevention

A dedicated component given falls are a "giant". Includes multifactorial risk assessment (medication, vision, footwear, home hazards, postural hypotension) and exercise targeting balance and strength (Otago-type programmes, Tai Chi).

#### 5.3 Cognitive rehabilitation

Cognitive stimulation, dual-task training, delirium prevention bundles, and environmental cueing for dementia.

#### 5.4 Psychosocial rehabilitation

Managing depression (exercise has antidepressant effect), reducing isolation, and supporting participation and meaningful activity.

#### 5.5 Nutritional and metabolic support

Protein-energy optimisation, vitamin D, coordinated with exercise for sarcopenia — the "exercise + protein" synergy.

#### 5.6 Continence, cardiopulmonary and pain rehabilitation

Pelvic-floor training; pulmonary rehab for COPD; cardiac rehab post-MI; management of chronic musculoskeletal pain (OA).

#### 5.7 Environmental and assistive-technology component

Home hazard modification, ramps, grab-bars, appropriate footwear, and prescription of walkers, wheelchairs and other assistive products (linking to WHO GATE/APL).

#### 5.8 Caregiver training and long-term-care support

Teaching safe transfers, positioning, exercise supervision and prevention of caregiver burnout.

### 6. Principles of Geriatric Rehabilitation

- **Start low, go slow, but do progress** — respect reduced reserve yet ensure adequate exercise dose.
- **Function first** — goals framed around ADL/participation.
- **Multimorbidity-aware** — integrate rather than treat diseases in isolation.
- **Avoid iatrogenesis and immobility** — early mobilisation, deprescribing.
- **Person-centred and dignity-preserving.**
- **Continuity** — from acute to post-acute to community with clear follow-up.

## Qc. Describe the development of a multi-component geriatric rehabilitation programme. Discuss its evidence base, components, dosage, progression and evaluation, with special reference to frailty, sarcopenia and falls. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

The single most robust intervention in geriatric rehabilitation is **multi-component exercise**. A **multi-component (or multimodal) geriatric rehabilitation programme** is a structured, individually tailored programme that combines several exercise/training modalities — typically **resistance (strength), balance, aerobic (endurance) and flexibility** training, often with **functional/task-specific** training and adjuncts such as **nutrition** and **cognitive** components — to target the multiple, simultaneously failing systems of the older adult. It contrasts with single-modality programmes and is the design of choice for **frailty**, **sarcopenia** and **falls** because these conditions are inherently multifactorial.

The rationale rests on the **frailty cycle**: sarcopenia and undernutrition beget weakness → slow gait and low activity → further deconditioning, falls and disability. A programme must therefore attack strength, power, balance, endurance and nutrition together.

Definitions:

- **Multi-component exercise programme:** combined resistance + balance + aerobic (± flexibility/functional) training in one prescription.
- **Progressive resistance training (PRT):** systematically increasing external load to overload muscle; the mainstay against sarcopenia.
- **Power training:** high-velocity resistance training; particularly important as power declines faster than strength and correlates with falls and functional performance.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **Principles of exercise prescription (FITT-VP):** **F**requency, **I**ntensity, **T**ime, **T**ype, **V**olume, **P**rogression — the ACSM structure adapted for older/frail adults.
- **Overload, specificity, reversibility and individualisation** — classic training principles, with reversibility especially relevant given rapid deconditioning in elders.
- **ICF** — programme goals mapped from impairment (strength) to activity (gait/ADL) to participation.
- **Behaviour-change theory** — adherence is the Achilles heel; use **Social Cognitive Theory**, self-efficacy building, goal-setting and self-monitoring to sustain participation.
- **RE-AIM** for programme-level evaluation and scale-up.

### 3. Evidence Base

- **Frailty:** systematic reviews and RCTs show multi-component/resistance-based exercise improves strength, gait speed, SPPB and can reverse or attenuate physical frailty.
- **Sarcopenia:** PRT (± protein) is the most effective intervention for muscle mass and strength (EWGSOP2/AWGS recommendations).
- **Falls:** Cochrane (Sherrington et al.) demonstrates that exercise reduces the **rate of falls by about 23%** in community-dwelling older adults, with **balance and functional exercises** the most effective category; programmes providing a **high dose (≥3 h/week) of challenging balance training** are most protective. Evidence-based programmes include **Otago Exercise Programme** and **Tai Chi**.
- **Cognition/mood:** exercise improves executive function and reduces depressive symptoms.

### 4. Steps in Developing the Programme

#### 4.1 Assessment and risk stratification

Perform CGA (Q b): grip strength, chair-rise/SPPB, gait speed, TUG, balance (Berg), frailty (Fried/CFS), falls history, cognition, nutrition (MNA/SARC-F), comorbidity screen and exercise safety clearance. Identify red flags (unstable angina, severe aortic stenosis, acute illness).

#### 4.2 Goal-setting

Collaborative, function-oriented SMART goals (e.g., "climb one flight of stairs unaided in 8 weeks"), using Goal Attainment Scaling.

#### 4.3 Prescription of components with dosage

The table gives evidence-based FITT parameters adapted for older/frail adults.

| Component | Frequency | Intensity | Time/Volume | Type/Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|-------------|-----------|
| Resistance (strength) | 2–3 days/week | Moderate→high (start 40–50% 1RM, progress to 70–80%); RPE 15–17 | 1–3 sets × 8–12 reps, 8–10 major muscle groups | Machines/bands/body-weight; foundation for sarcopenia |
| Power training | 2–3 days/week | Moderate load, **high velocity** on concentric phase | 1–3 sets × 6–10 reps | Improves falls-relevant rapid force; add once base strength gained |
| Balance | ≥3 days/week (aim ≥3 h/week total challenging balance) | Progressively challenging (reduce base, perturbations) | Integrated into sessions | Tandem stance/walk, weight shifts, reactive stepping, Tai Chi |
| Aerobic/endurance | ≥3–5 days/week | Moderate (RPE 12–14; talk test) | Accumulate ≥150 min/week; start 10-min bouts | Walking, cycling, aquatic |
| Flexibility | ≥2 days/week | To mild tension | 10–30 s holds, 2–4 reps | Major muscle-tendon groups |
| Functional/task-specific | Each session | Task-relevant | Repetition of ADL tasks | Sit-to-stand, stairs, floor transfer, gait |

Add **nutritional** guidance (≥1.0–1.2 g/kg/day protein, more in acute illness/sarcopenia; vitamin D if deficient) and a **cognitive/dual-task** element where appropriate.

#### 4.4 Progression

Apply gradual overload: increase load when the client completes the top of the rep range with good form (e.g., "2-for-2 rule"), progressively reduce balance support and increase task difficulty. Re-assess every 4–6 weeks and re-dose.

#### 4.5 Safety, supervision and adaptation

Warm-up/cool-down, monitor BP/symptoms, fall-safe environment, and adapt for comorbidities (OA — reduce impact; osteoporosis — avoid loaded flexion; cognitive impairment — simplify cueing, involve caregiver). Supervised sessions initially, transitioning to supported home practice.

#### 4.6 Adherence and behaviour change

Use goal-setting, self-monitoring diaries, group/social components, telephone/tele-follow-up, and caregiver involvement to sustain the programme beyond the supervised phase.

#### 4.7 Evaluation

Re-measure SPPB, gait speed, grip, TUG, Berg, falls rate, frailty status, ADL and quality of life; evaluate the programme with RE-AIM (reach, adherence, effectiveness, maintenance).

*Figure: The programme can be pictured as four interlocking gears — strength, balance, endurance and flexibility — driven by a nutrition/behaviour-change "engine", together turning the wheel of improved function and reduced falls.*

### 5. Structuring a Session and a Cycle

A typical 45–60 min session: warm-up (5–10 min) → balance (10–15 min) → resistance/power (15–20 min) → aerobic (10–15 min) → flexibility/cool-down (5–10 min). A programme cycle typically runs **8–12 weeks supervised**, then a **maintenance** phase (home/community group) because gains reverse rapidly if discontinued (reversibility).

### 6. Special Applications

- **Frail/very frail (CFS 6–7):** begin with chair-based, low-load, high-supervision, functional strength and sit-to-stand; progress cautiously.
- **Post-hospital deconditioning:** early mobilisation and reconditioning to prevent hospital-associated disability.
- **Falls clinic:** Otago/Tai Chi-based high-dose balance programme plus multifactorial risk modification.

<div class="callout callout-india">

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

Multi-component programmes must be adapted to Indian realities: floor-based living (need for **floor-transfer training** — getting up from and down to the floor for toileting/prayer), squatting cultural postures, limited gym access, and low baseline physical-activity literacy. Low-cost, equipment-light designs are ideal — **body-weight resistance, resistance bands, water bottles/sandbags as weights, chair-based programmes, and Yoga/Tai-Chi-style balance work**. **Yoga**, culturally acceptable and increasingly evidence-supported for balance and flexibility in Indian elders, can be integrated (promoted nationally through AYUSH). Delivery can leverage **NPHCE physiotherapy units** and District Hospital rehab, **HWC/Ayushman Arogya Mandir** wellness sessions, community **senior-citizen groups** and **anganwadi/community halls**. Nutrition guidance must address widespread **protein and vitamin-D inadequacy** and vegetarian diets (pulses, dairy, protein adequacy). Given the therapist shortage, ASHAs and trained volunteers can supervise group exercise under PT guidance, with periodic PT/tele-review. Programmes should also be sensitive to **out-of-pocket cost** and gender norms limiting older women's outdoor participation (favouring home/group-based models).
</div>

<div class="callout callout-advance">

**🔬 Recent Advances (2020–2026)**

- **Sherrington Cochrane update (2019) and subsequent syntheses** reaffirm exercise reduces falls ~23%, with balance/functional and high-dose programmes most effective — the current gold-standard evidence for programme design.
- **Power (high-velocity) training** emphasised over conventional slow PRT for falls and functional outcomes in recent trials.
- **Exercise + protein/vitamin-D multidomain trials** (e.g., building on **SPRINTT** and DO-HEALTH) support combining resistance exercise with nutrition for physical frailty/sarcopenia.
- **Tele-rehabilitation and app/wearable-guided home exercise:** post-COVID growth of remotely supervised multi-component programmes with video and wearable adherence tracking; feasible and effective in community elders.
- **Digital falls and gait monitoring:** wearable inertial sensors provide objective gait speed, sway and fall detection to titrate and evaluate programmes remotely.
- **WHO 2020 physical activity guidelines** explicitly recommend **multicomponent physical activity emphasising balance and strength ≥3 days/week** for older adults to prevent falls.
</div>

### Exam Key Points (rapid revision)

- Multi-component = **resistance + balance + aerobic + flexibility** (± power, functional, nutrition, cognitive); design of choice for frailty, sarcopenia, falls.
- Target the **frailty cycle**; use **FITT-VP** and principles of overload/specificity/individualisation.
- **Resistance:** 2–3×/wk, 8–12 reps, progress to 70–80% 1RM; add **power** (high velocity) for falls.
- **Balance:** ≥3×/wk, aim **≥3 h/week challenging balance** (most protective against falls); Otago, Tai Chi.
- **Aerobic:** ≥150 min/wk moderate; **flexibility** 2×/wk.
- Evidence: exercise cuts **falls ~23%** (Sherrington/Cochrane); PRT + protein best for sarcopenia.
- **Progress** by re-assessment every 4–6 wks; include **maintenance** phase (reversibility).
- **Nutrition:** protein ≥1.0–1.2 g/kg/day + vitamin D if deficient.
- India: low-cost, chair/band/yoga-based, **floor-transfer training**, group/home delivery via HWC/NPHCE, address protein/vit-D gaps.

## Qd. Discuss strategies to improve geriatric rehabilitation service provision within the healthcare delivery system. Address health-system building blocks, integration, workforce, financing and quality, with reference to WHO Rehabilitation 2030. (20 marks)

### 1. Introduction & Definitions

Improving *service provision* means strengthening the health system so that older people can actually access timely, integrated, affordable, quality geriatric rehabilitation. WHO estimates that **globally ~2.4 billion people** live with a condition benefiting from rehabilitation, and that need is rising sharply with ageing, yet rehabilitation is among the most neglected health services in LMICs — the gap that **WHO's Rehabilitation 2030 initiative (launched 2017)** and the **Package of Interventions for Rehabilitation (PIR)** seek to close.

**Health service delivery**, in WHO terms, is one of six **health-system building blocks** (service delivery, health workforce, information, medical products/technologies, financing, leadership/governance). Improving geriatric rehabilitation requires action across all six, plus deliberate **integration** into the continuum of care and the **UHC** agenda.

Definitions:

- **Rehabilitation 2030:** WHO call to action to scale up rehabilitation within health systems as an essential health service under UHC.
- **Integrated people-centred health services (IPCHS):** the WHO framework for coordinating care around people's needs across levels and life course.
- **Continuum of care:** the seamless flow from acute → post-acute/sub-acute → community/home rehabilitation with clear referral and follow-up.

### 2. Conceptual / Theoretical Framework

- **WHO six building blocks** — the organising skeleton for any service-strengthening answer.
- **Rehabilitation 2030 & Systematic Assessment of Rehabilitation Situation (STARS)/Guide for Action (GRASP)** — WHO tools to assess and plan rehabilitation system strengthening.
- **WHO IPCHS framework** — five strategies: empowering people/communities; strengthening governance/accountability; reorienting the model of care (towards primary/community & prevention); coordinating services; creating an enabling environment.
- **ICOPE / intrinsic-capacity model** — the community-level integration vehicle for older people.
- **WHO Health Workforce (rehabilitation competency framework)** and **Innovative Care for Chronic Conditions (ICCC)** model.
- **Donabedian (structure–process–outcome)** and continuous **quality improvement** for quality assurance.

### 3. Strategies by Building Block

| Building block | Current gaps (esp. LMICs/India) | Strategies to improve geriatric rehabilitation provision |
|----------------|-------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Service delivery | Fragmented, acute-focused, urban-tertiary bias | Integrate rehab into primary care (ICOPE at HWCs); develop referral pathways & post-acute pathways; home-based & tele-rehab; age-friendly facilities |
| Health workforce | Few geriatricians/geriatric PTs; maldistribution | Expand PT/OT training, geriatric curricula; task-shifting to CHWs/mid-level; CPD; rehabilitation assistants |
| Information systems | Poor age/disability-disaggregated data; no rehab registry | Integrate rehab data into HMIS/ABDM; use ICF/ICHI coding; outcome registries |
| Medical products/AT | Assistive products scarce/unaffordable | National assistive-products list; RVY-type schemes; local production; procurement |
| Financing | High out-of-pocket; rehab under-insured | Include rehab in benefit packages (PM-JAY); reduce OOP; domiciliary care funding |
| Leadership/governance | Rehab not prioritised; weak multisector links | National rehab strategy/focal point; intersectoral (health–social welfare) coordination; Rehabilitation 2030 alignment |

### 4. Integration and Reorienting the Model of Care

The pivotal strategy is to **shift from a hospital-centric, disease-focused model to an integrated, primary-care-anchored, community-oriented model**:

- **Integrate rehabilitation into primary health care** using ICOPE screening and care plans at HWCs/PHCs.
- **Build the continuum of care:** structured acute → post-acute (sub-acute geriatric rehab units) → community/home rehab with defined referral criteria, discharge planning and follow-up to prevent readmission and hospital-associated disability.
- **Care coordination / case management** for complex frail multimorbid elders (single care coordinator, shared care plan).
- **Intermediate care and "hospital-at-home"** models to bridge acute and community.

### 5. Workforce Strengthening and Task-shifting

Given the acute shortage of geriatricians and geriatric-trained therapists, strategies include: embedding **geriatrics and geriatric rehabilitation in PT/OT/medical/nursing curricula**; creating **rehabilitation assistants/mid-level workers**; **task-shifting** routine screening and supervised exercise to ASHAs/CHWs/family caregivers under professional supervision; **continuing professional development** and **tele-mentoring** (ECHO-model) to build rural capacity; and appropriate **regulation** (in India, the **National Commission for Allied and Healthcare Professions (NCAHP) Act, 2021** professionalises and standardises physiotherapy and rehabilitation cadres).

### 6. Financing and Access

Reduce catastrophic out-of-pocket spending by **including rehabilitation and assistive products in insurance benefit packages**, funding **domiciliary and community rehabilitation**, and subsidising devices for the poor. Ensure **geographic and physical accessibility** (age-friendly, barrier-free facilities; outreach for the homebound).

### 7. Quality, Information and Governance

- **Quality:** standards, clinical pathways/guidelines, outcome measurement (Barthel, SPPB, goal attainment), audit and QI (Donabedian; PDSA).
- **Information:** ICF-based documentation, rehabilitation indicators in the HMIS, registries.
- **Governance:** a national rehabilitation strategy and focal point, **intersectoral coordination** between health and social-welfare ministries (crucial for elder care), and alignment with **Rehabilitation 2030** and the **Decade of Healthy Ageing**.

*Figure: A "house" diagram can be described — the roof (healthy ageing / UHC goals) supported by six pillars (the building blocks), standing on a foundation of governance and financing, with integration/continuity as the connecting beams.*

<div class="callout callout-india">

**🇮🇳 Indian Context**

India's service-strengthening scaffolding for geriatric rehabilitation includes: **NPHCE** (tiered geriatric services with physiotherapy units from District Hospital to sub-centre, Regional Geriatric Centres for training); **Ayushman Bharat** with **Health and Wellness Centres/Ayushman Arogya Mandirs** delivering **comprehensive primary care including elderly screening**, and **PM-JAY (Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana)** offering hospitalisation cover that can reduce out-of-pocket costs (though outpatient rehab coverage remains a gap). The **Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM)** and **eSanjeevani** tele-consultation platform enable tele-geriatrics and integration of records. The **NCAHP Act, 2021** creates a statutory framework standardising physiotherapy and rehabilitation education and practice, strengthening the workforce block. The **Rashtriya Vayoshri Yojana** and **ADIP scheme** address assistive products; the **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007** and the **National Policy on Older Persons / National Policy for Senior Citizens** provide governance and intersectoral mandate. Persisting gaps that a good answer should name: acute shortage of geriatricians and geriatric physiotherapists, weak post-acute/long-term-care infrastructure, urban-tertiary concentration, limited outpatient rehab financing, and poor age-disaggregated rehabilitation data — addressable through task-shifting, tele-rehab, curriculum reform and integrating rehab indicators into the HMIS/ABDM.
</div>

<div class="callout callout-advance">

**🔬 Recent Advances (2020–2026)**

- **WHO Package of Interventions for Rehabilitation (PIR):** launched from 2021, providing evidence-based rehabilitation intervention packages (including for ageing-related conditions, osteoarthritis, stroke, fractures) to help ministries plan services.
- **WHO Rehabilitation Competency Framework (RCF, 2020)** to guide workforce development.
- **World Rehabilitation Alliance (WHO, 2022)** advocacy network to embed rehabilitation in health systems and emergencies.
- **Tele-rehabilitation scale-up:** post-COVID national tele-platforms (India's **eSanjeevani**) extend specialist rehab and follow-up to rural elders; strong evidence base emerging for tele-rehab non-inferiority in selected conditions.
- **Project ECHO tele-mentoring** for building rural geriatric/rehab capacity.
- **ABDM/digital health** enabling integrated longitudinal records and remote monitoring for continuity of care.
- **Rehabilitation 2030 country implementation** — increasing number of national rehabilitation strategic plans using **STARS/GRASP** situational-assessment tools.
</div>

### Exam Key Points (rapid revision)

- Frame with **WHO six building blocks** + **Rehabilitation 2030 / PIR** + **IPCHS** integration strategies.
- Core shift: **hospital-centric → integrated, primary-care-anchored, community-oriented** model (ICOPE at primary care).
- Build the **continuum of care:** acute → post-acute/sub-acute → community/home with referral, discharge planning, follow-up; add intermediate care/hospital-at-home.
- **Workforce:** geriatric curricula, mid-level workers, **task-shifting**, CPD, tele-mentoring (ECHO); India's **NCAHP Act 2021**.
- **Financing:** include rehab & AT in benefit packages, cut OOP, fund domiciliary care (PM-JAY gaps in outpatient rehab).
- **Information/quality/governance:** ICF-based data in HMIS/ABDM, registries, national rehab strategy, health–social-welfare intersectoral coordination.
- India: **NPHCE, HWC/Ayushman Arogya Mandir, PM-JAY, ABDM, eSanjeevani, RVY, NCAHP Act, Senior Citizens Act, National Policy for Senior Citizens**.
- 2020–26: **PIR, RCF 2020, World Rehabilitation Alliance, tele-rehab, ECHO**.
